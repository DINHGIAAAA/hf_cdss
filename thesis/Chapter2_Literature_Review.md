# CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORY

This chapter introduces the concepts, clinical background, and technologies that underpin the heart-failure clinical decision support system (HF-CDSS) developed in this thesis. The goal is to give a reader who is not an expert in medicine or artificial intelligence enough context to understand why the system is designed the way it is. Each technique is explained in plain language: what it is, what it is used for, and how it helps in practice. Citations follow the numbering in References.md.

## 2.1 Clinical Decision Support Systems

A Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) is software that helps clinicians, pharmacists, or patients make better health decisions. The Institute of Medicine defines CDSS as any electronic system designed to aid directly in clinical decision making [16]. In everyday terms, a CDSS does not replace the doctor. It organizes relevant facts, checks for safety problems, and suggests options so the clinician can decide faster and with fewer oversights. For chronic diseases such as heart failure, the value often lies in repeated visits over months: spotting missing medications, flagging unsafe combinations, and reminding the clinician about lab monitoring after a dose change.

CDSS tools differ in how and when they intervene. Passive systems wait for the user to ask a question, such as looking up a drug interaction. Active systems push alerts into the workflow, for example warning before a prescription is signed. They also differ in mechanism. Rule-based systems use explicit if-then logic that can be traced step by step. Machine-learning systems learn patterns from large datasets but are often harder to explain. Knowledge-base and retrieval systems ground answers in curated documents and structured facts. Modern heart-failure support increasingly combines these approaches: deterministic rules for safety, structured knowledge for relationships between drugs and labs, and language models for reading free text and writing clear explanations.

The history of CDSS shows recurring themes of transparency and workflow fit. MYCIN (1976) was an early rule-based system for infectious disease therapy at Stanford. It encoded clinical heuristics as production rules, asked structured questions, and explained its reasoning [15]. MYCIN never entered routine clinical use, but it proved that medical logic could be formalized and that clinicians need to see why a recommendation was made. Later systems such as DXplain and Quick Medical Reference extended diagnostic support across broader medical domains. From the 1990s onward, drug-allergy checks and dosing calculators became common inside commercial electronic health records, though many alerts were ignored because they were too frequent or too vague.

More recent projects applied knowledge graphs and predictive models at larger scale. IBM Watson for Oncology combined literature and pathway knowledge with patient summaries, illustrating both the promise and the limits of knowledge-driven support when curation and local practice norms are weak. The current generation adds retrieval-augmented generation and graph-augmented retrieval so clinicians can ask questions in natural language while the system tries to cite authoritative sources. Across every generation, the same lesson appears: accuracy alone is not enough. A correct recommendation that arrives too late, in the wrong format, or buried in a queue of false alarms will not change care.

Osheroff's "Five Rights" framework captures this workflow perspective [17]. Effective CDSS must deliver the right information to the right person in the right format through the right channel at the right time. For heart-failure guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), the right information includes the patient's heart-failure type, current drug classes, renal function, potassium level, blood pressure, and timing rules when switching between related drugs. The right person is usually the prescribing physician or clinical pharmacist. The right format may combine simple status labels (continue, consider, avoid) with a short rationale and links to guideline or label text. The right channel might be an electronic health record alert, a dedicated dashboard, or a conversational interface. The right time is when medication decisions are actually made, such as at follow-up or medication reconciliation, not hours later in an unread inbox.

Quantitative metrics complement the Five Rights. Sensitivity measures how often the system catches true problems. Specificity measures how often it correctly ignores situations that are actually safe. Positive predictive value (PPV) asks, of all alerts fired, how many were truly needed. When PPV is low, clinicians start overriding alerts, a phenomenon called alert fatigue. For heart-failure CDSS, a missed hard contraindication (for example, starting an ARNI too soon after an ACE inhibitor) can harm the patient, so safety checks aim for high sensitivity. At the same time, soft cautions that fire on every stable patient erode trust. Good design therefore tiers alerts by severity, suppresses duplicates, and pairs numeric metrics with usability evaluation [4], [5].

Heart failure adds domain-specific demands that generic drug checkers often miss. Therapy is organized by drug class pillars (RAAS inhibitors, beta blockers, MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors), not single-drug optimization. Many rules depend on labs and vitals rather than absolute bans. Temporal rules govern class transitions, such as the washout period between ACE inhibitors and ARNIs. GDMT underuse is often a process problem as much as a knowledge problem: clinicians may know the guideline but defer titration because of hypotension, hyperkalemia, or limited visit time. Bilingual and low-resource settings add further complexity through brand names, mixed-language notes, and local terminology. These challenges motivate the hybrid architecture developed later in this thesis.

## 2.2 Knowledge Graphs

A Knowledge Graph (KG) is a way of storing knowledge as a network of things (entities) and connections (relationships) [7]. Instead of burying facts inside long documents, a graph makes structure explicit: which drug treats which condition, which drug interacts with which other drug, and which lab value triggers a caution. This matters in medicine because clinical reasoning is relational. A question about whether spironolactone is safe in a patient on lisinopril with reduced kidney function requires linking drug-drug interaction, renal threshold, and electrolyte risk. Those facts may appear in different sections of different documents. A well-built graph binds them to canonical drug and lab nodes so software can traverse the connections at query time.

The basic building block is often a triple: subject, predicate, object. Examples include (Bisoprolol, treats, Heart_Failure), (Sacubitril/Valsartan, contraindicated_with, ACE_inhibitor), and (MRA, requires_monitoring, serum_potassium). Nodes represent typed entities such as drugs, drug classes, diseases, and lab concepts. Edges represent relations such as treats, indicated_for, contraindicated_with, and interacts_with. Properties attach numbers and text to nodes or edges, such as starting dose 1.25 mg, washout window 36 hours, or monitoring interval one week after MRA initiation. Axioms express conditional logic that plain triples cannot capture cleanly, for example "do not combine ACE inhibitor with ARNI within 36 hours of the last ACE inhibitor dose." In this thesis, Neo4j stores entity-relation structure while PostgreSQL holds executable rules with approval workflow, splitting exploratory graph traversal from governed production logic.

Entity linking (also called normalization) maps surface text to canonical graph nodes. A patient chart may say "Entresto," "sacubitril/valsartan," or a local brand name. Without linking, the same medication appears as disconnected nodes and interaction checking fails silently. Linking pipelines combine dictionaries, fuzzy matching, embedding similarity for near synonyms, and disambiguation when one string could mean several concepts. At ingestion, drug mentions from FDA Structured Product Labels and guidelines are normalized to RxNorm-aligned keys before graph import. At query time, the intake pipeline performs similar normalization on free-text medication lists.

Multi-hop path traversal follows chains of relations to answer questions that no single edge resolves. Starting from a patient's ACE inhibitor, the graph might traverse contraindicated_with to ARNI, then indicated_for to HFrEF, revealing both why a washout period matters and why ARNI remains valuable after the wait. Graph query languages such as Cypher (used by Neo4j) express these paths declaratively. Returned subgraphs become structured evidence for downstream language models and verification agents. Path structure also supports regression tests during construction: if no path connects ACE inhibitor to ARNI via contraindicated_with, a critical label constraint may have been missed during ingestion.

Biomedical knowledge resources provide vocabulary and pharmacology that graphs can integrate [8]. The Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) links synonymous terms across vocabularies [9]. SNOMED CT offers hierarchical clinical concepts [10]. DrugBank and PubChem supply pharmacology and interaction annotations [11]. RxNorm standardizes medication names for prescribing systems. Disease ontologies excel at classification; FDA labels and society guidelines supply numeric dosing, boxed warnings, and conditional language. HF-CDSS draws primarily on DailyMed SPL XML for agent-level detail and ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines for class-level GDMT recommendations [2], [3]. The graph does not replicate entire terminologies; scope is heart-failure pharmacotherapy, but the same normalization principles apply when scaling to hospital-wide deployments.

In CDSS, knowledge graphs support drug-disease-lab reasoning, multi-hop retrieval over clinical pathways, and contextual evidence that pure keyword or embedding search may miss when facts are scattered across sections. For heart failure, the graph encodes not only which classes treat HFrEF but also cross-class constraints that flat document retrieval may omit. Graph retrieval in HF-CDSS runs in parallel with dense and sparse chunk retrieval, with ranks fused before generation, developing the GraphRAG pattern described in Section 2.5.

## 2.3 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines search with language-model generation so answers are grounded in external evidence rather than the model's internal memory alone [12]. Large language models learn broad patterns from training text, but they do not guarantee current label wording, local formulary rules, or patient-specific safety. In medicine, that gap is dangerous: a fluent but outdated dose or a missing contraindication can mislead a busy clinician. RAG reframes the language model as a reader and summarizer over a curated knowledge base that can be updated independently of model weights. When guidelines expand SGLT2 inhibitor use in HFrEF, updating indexed chunks and graph edges refreshes system behavior without retraining the generator.

RAG typically involves indexing documents as chunks, embedding them into vectors, retrieving the most relevant chunks for a user query, and prompting the language model to answer using only that retrieved context. Two retrieval families are commonly combined.

Dense retrieval uses embedding models (bi-encoders) that map queries and documents into a shared numerical space. Similar meaning yields similar vectors even when wording differs. For example, "reduced EF" and "low ejection fraction" may sit close together, as may "ARNI" and "neprilysin inhibitor." At index time, document vectors are precomputed; at query time, nearest-neighbor search returns semantically similar passages quickly. Models such as BGE-M3 and MedCPT are used in clinical settings for this purpose. Dense retrieval handles synonyms and paraphrase well but can miss rare exact tokens such as brand names, numeric cutoffs, or regulatory phrases like "36 hours."

Sparse retrieval uses lexical statistics instead of neural embeddings at query time. BM25 (Best Matching 25) is the standard sparse ranker. It scores documents by how well query terms overlap document terms, with adjustments so very long documents do not win merely because they contain more words, and repeated terms do not boost score without limit. BM25 excels at exact entity mentions and regulatory language but weakly connects conceptually related text with different vocabulary. Clinical questions often mix both needs: semantic intent ("Can I start MRA in advanced CKD?") and lexical anchors (spironolactone, eGFR 22, potassium 5.6). Hybrid pipelines therefore run dense and sparse retrievers in parallel and merge their ranked lists.

Chunking splits long guidelines and labels into retrieval-sized pieces. Naive fixed-length splits break clinical sentences mid-thought, separating a condition from its consequence. Sentence-aware chunking with controlled overlap keeps local context intact. Section headers from SPL XML or guideline outlines provide natural boundaries and support provenance: retrieved passages can cite source document and section code for verification. Vector stores such as ChromaDB persist embeddings with metadata filters. Index structures like HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) enable fast approximate nearest-neighbor search over large corpora by navigating a multi-layer graph of vectors, trading exact recall for speed acceptable when only the top few results matter.

Healthcare RAG faces strict accuracy requirements, specialized terminology, multi-factor patient context, and continuous knowledge updates. RAG does not eliminate error; it shifts failure modes. Retrieval omission means the right passage was not in the top results. Mis-ranking means a generic overview outranks a specific contraindiction. Misinterpretation means the language model ignores retrieved text. Clinical CDSS therefore pairs RAG with deterministic safety rules and human review. In HF-CDSS, verification agents check that cited chunk identifiers were actually retrieved and that hard-block constraints were evaluated by the rule engine, not inferred by the language model alone.

## 2.4 Large Language Models

Large language models (LLMs) are neural networks trained on vast text corpora to predict and generate language. They contain billions of parameters and can draft clinical prose, extract structured fields from narratives, and answer medical questions in natural language. Milestones include the Transformer architecture (2017) [18], which introduced scalable attention mechanisms, and GPT-3 (2020) [19], which showed that scale plus instruction tuning produces general-purpose models capable of following natural-language directives. In CDSS, LLMs are best suited to explanation, semantic extraction, query expansion, and verification, not unbounded autonomous prescribing. HF-CDSS uses locally hosted Qwen2.5 models via Ollama for clinician-facing answers and lighter tasks such as HyDE expansion and borderline section review.

The Transformer relies on attention: each token in a sequence can weight how much other tokens matter when forming its representation. Multi-head attention runs several attention patterns in parallel, allowing the model to relate distant pieces of context, such as connecting "eGFR 22" with "renal impairment" across a long clinical note. Stacked layers, feed-forward sublayers, and residual connections enable training at scale. These models store broad medical facts implicitly in their weights, which creates both capability and risk: they may fluently state incorrect doses or invent unsupported interactions.

Prompting techniques steer model behavior without changing weights. Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to show intermediate reasoning steps before a final answer, which can help multi-step clinical tasks at the cost of latency. Few-shot prompting supplies example input-output pairs in the prompt to steer format and tone. Human-in-the-loop (HITL) workflows keep physicians as final decision authority: the system proposes drafts and surfaces evidence rather than placing orders autonomously. HF-CDSS applies HITL at rule approval, chat recommendations, and optional dismissal of non-blocking alerts.

Domain-adapted models such as BioBERT and ClinicalBERT improve biomedical entity recognition and question answering relative to general models. For retrieval, clinical bi-encoders align embedding space with medical question-passage pairs. Medical LLMs nonetheless suffer from knowledge cutoffs, underrepresentation of non-English corpora, and uneven coverage of local prescribing practices. HF-CDSS mitigates these limits through RAG over locally ingested labels and guidelines and through deterministic GDMT engines that do not depend on parametric memory for core safety.

Hallucination, producing fluent but unsupported statements, is the primary LLM risk in clinical use. Intrinsic hallucinations contradict provided context. Extrinsic hallucinations introduce facts not present anywhere in the retrieved evidence. Mitigations layered in HF-CDSS include mandatory citations to retrieved chunk identifiers, structured output schemas validated by Pydantic, deterministic post-checks via rule engines and verification agents, tiered safety classification (hard_block versus consider_with_caution), and mandatory human review before action. Local inference via Ollama supports air-gapped or privacy-sensitive environments where cloud API routing is unacceptable, keeping patient text on premises while accepting operator responsibility for hardware sizing and model updates.

## 2.5 GraphRAG

GraphRAG augments retrieval and generation with graph structure so the system can answer questions that require relational context, not just similar text [13]. Vector RAG retrieves passages that look semantically like the query. That works well for "What titration schedule does the label recommend for bisoprolol in HFrEF?" It works poorly when the answer requires linking two entities through a typed relationship, such as "Why must I wait before switching from ramipril to Entresto?" That question needs a contraindication edge between ACE inhibitor and ARNI with a temporal washout property. GraphRAG does not replace vector RAG; it complements it by supplying structured facts and subgraphs that are fused with textual evidence before generation.

Compared with vector-only RAG, graph-augmented retrieval offers stronger relationship understanding and natural multi-hop retrieval, but at higher engineering cost. Vector RAG excels at semantic similarity with simpler infrastructure. GraphRAG additionally retrieves structured facts and subgraphs, then merges them with text chunks. HF-CDSS implements a pragmatic subset: local entity-centric Neo4j traversal plus hybrid chunk retrieval, without full offline community summarization at initial deployment.

Two search modes appear in the GraphRAG literature. Local search starts from entities mentioned in the patient profile or query (medications, conditions, abnormal labs) and expands their neighborhood in the graph. For heart failure, local expansion from spironolactone might retrieve interaction edges to ACE inhibitors, contraindication predicates tied to eGFR and potassium, and class membership in MRA. Local search is patient-specific and supports personalized safety checks. Global search, in community-based GraphRAG variants, uses precomputed summaries of densely connected subgraph clusters to answer thematic questions such as "What is the standard HFrEF pharmacotherapy bundle?" without anchoring on a single drug node. HF-CDSS emphasizes local search and deterministic GDMT engines because gap analysis and safety checks are patient-specific; global summaries remain an extensibility path for educational overviews.

Hybrid retrieval merges ranked lists from multiple retrievers using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). RRF assigns each document a score based on its rank in each retriever's list, with a smoothing constant (commonly k = 60) so top ranks from any single retriever still contribute. RRF avoids calibrating incompatible raw scores when cosine similarities from dense search and BM25 lexical scores are combined. Dense retrieval itself often uses cosine similarity between L2-normalized query and document embeddings from a bi-encoder.

Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) address vocabulary mismatch between short clinical questions and formal label prose [14]. A small language model generates a hypothetical answer document from the query; that hypothetical text is embedded and used for dense retrieval. The hypothetical passage may sit closer in embedding space to true guideline wording than the original brief question ("start ARNI?"). HyDE can misfire if the hypothetical document invents incorrect facts, which is why HF-CDSS combines HyDE with BM25, graph facts, deterministic rules, and verification rather than trusting HyDE alone.

Optional second-stage reranking applies a more expensive scorer to the top of the fused candidate list. Cross-encoders jointly encode query and passage in one forward pass and discriminate relevance more finely than bi-encoders, but they cannot precompute document representations for an entire corpus. The standard pattern is bi-encoder for recall, reranker for precision at the top of the list. Query decomposition splits compound questions into sub-queries retrieved independently before fusion, reducing the problem of one embedding trying to represent multiple intents at once.

GraphRAG fits heart-failure CDSS because drug-disease-lab knowledge is inherently relational. Clinical reasoning is often multi-step: washout, then class switch, then dose titration. Patient context spans interacting variables. Graph structures can extend when guidelines update without re-embedding entire corpora. HF-CDSS runs GraphRAG primarily as an evidence and explanation layer parallel to a deterministic GDMT rule engine. Structured recommendation status comes from PostgreSQL rules; GraphRAG supplies passages and graph facts the language model must cite when explaining why.

## 2.6 Medical Knowledge Construction

Building a medical knowledge graph for CDSS requires combining structured and unstructured sources, extracting entities and relations, linking them to standard identifiers, and governing quality before production use. Medical graphs feature diverse entity types (drugs, classes, conditions, labs), rich relation vocabularies (contraindication, dose adjustment, monitoring, indicated_for), numeric properties (doses in mg, thresholds in mEq/L or mL/min/1.73 m²), temporal constraints (36-hour washout, one-week potassium recheck), and graded evidence reliability. Heart-failure graphs must reconcile guideline narrative about four GDMT pillars with FDA label numeric detail for each agent. When guideline strength and label wording differ, governance workflows mark rules as draft until clinical leads resolve discrepancies [8].

Construction draws on structured sources (DrugBank, UMLS/SNOMED, FDA SPL XML, RxNorm) and unstructured sources (ESC/AHA/HFSA guidelines, trials, literature). HF-CDSS ingests DailyMed SPL labels and society guideline documents into versioned artifacts before PostgreSQL governance and Neo4j import. The typical pipeline proceeds from document parsing and section extraction through terminology normalization, named entity recognition (NER) for drugs, conditions, labs, and populations, relation extraction (RE) for contraindications, interactions, indications, and dose adjustments, entity linking to canonical identifiers, knowledge fusion to merge duplicates and resolve conflicts, and quality assurance with expert review before rules reach approved status.

Named entity recognition targets drug names and classes, conditions such as HFrEF and CKD, laboratory names and values, and population qualifiers. Relation extraction maps natural-language statements to typed edges: "avoid concurrent use" becomes contraindicated_with; "reduce starting dose in renal impairment" becomes dose_adjustment with an eGFR predicate; "monitor serum potassium" becomes requires_monitoring. LLM-assisted extraction with JSON schema constraints supplements pattern-based extractors on ambiguous guideline sentences. Quality assurance includes contradiction detection, orphan entity reports, and spot checks by clinical leads.

Downstream sync splits artifacts by purpose. Constraint, dose, interaction, and GDMT policy rules go to PostgreSQL with version and approval columns. Entity-relation bundles go to Neo4j. Chunks go to ChromaDB with metadata linking back to source identifiers. This multi-store pattern matches how different operations optimize: SQL filters for governed rules, graph traversal for relational exploration, vector search for free-text evidence.

Section filtering during ingestion uses a three-tier cascade aligned with cost-aware clinical NLP design. Tier 1 applies high-precision keyword matching on clinical headings and SPL section codes (DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION, WARNINGS AND PRECAUTIONS, CONTRAINDICATIONS, DRUG INTERACTIONS). This tier is fast, deterministic, and incurs no GPU cost. Tier 2 scores remaining sections with dense semantic similarity: section text is embedded and compared to prototype embeddings of clinical section types. Sections above a keep threshold are retained without language-model review. Tier 3 sends borderline sections to a small LLM for binary clinical-relevance classification; sections below a low threshold are dropped as likely administrative boilerplate. This cascade minimizes LLM calls while preserving recall, embodying a general principle: deploy expensive models only where cheaper methods abstain.

## 2.7 Heart Failure Clinical Foundations

Computer-science readers need enough clinical context to understand why HF-CDSS rules and retrieval scopes are shaped as they are. Heart failure is a syndrome of structural or functional cardiac abnormality with symptoms and signs. Classification by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) separates HF with reduced EF (HFrEF, typically LVEF ≤ 40%), mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), and preserved EF (HFpEF, typically LVEF ≥ 50%). Contemporary GDMT pillars have the strongest outcome evidence in HFrEF [2], [3]. HF-CDSS focuses on HFrEF GDMT optimization where class-level rules and labeling are most standardized. Phenotype gates recommendations: the intake pipeline extracts LVEF when documented and derives HFrEF eligibility before suggesting ARNI or certain class starts.

The four pillars of HFrEF GDMT are (1) renin-angiotensin system inhibition via ACE inhibitor, ARB, or ARNI; (2) evidence-based beta blockers (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate); (3) mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone); and (4) SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) [2], [3], [6]. Each reduces morbidity and mortality through partially overlapping hemodynamic and neurohormonal mechanisms. ACE inhibitors and ARBs block angiotensin II effects; ARNI combines valsartan with sacubitril, a neprilysin inhibitor that elevates natriuretic peptides, with superior outcome evidence over ACE inhibitor alone in pivotal trials but strict washout rules when switching from ACE inhibitor. Beta blockers antagonize chronic sympathetic activation in stable HFrEF; they require gradual uptitration and are avoided in acute decompensation until stabilization. MRAs reduce remodeling and hospitalization but increase hyperkalemia risk, especially with concurrent RAAS blockade. SGLT2 inhibitors reduce HF hospitalizations and cardiovascular death in HFrEF even in non-diabetic patients. CDSS must reason at class level ("missing MRA") while resolving to agent-specific doses from SPL catalogs.

Key laboratories and vitals drive eligibility and monitoring. Renal function (eGFR) gates MRA use and dose adjustment for many agents. Serum potassium monitoring is essential when combining RAAS inhibitors with MRAs; thresholds near 5.0 to 5.5 mEq/L often trigger caution or hold per label and guideline excerpts. Blood pressure limits uptitration of RAAS agents and beta blockers; symptomatic hypotension may require dose delay or diuretic adjustment before GDMT intensification. Heart rate affects beta-blocker tolerance; bradycardia may cap dose. HF-CDSS extracts these fields via hybrid intake, derives eGFR from creatinine when needed using deterministic formulas, and feeds values into constraint and dose engines rather than relying on the language model alone.

A distinctive HFrEF constraint is the mandatory washout period between ACE inhibitor and ARNI initiation, typically 36 hours, because combined ACE inhibition with neprilysin inhibition increases angioedema risk [2], [3]. This is a temporal rule, not a permanent contraindication: ARNI is appropriate after washout in eligible HFrEF. CDSS must capture last ACE inhibitor dose timing when available and emit hard-block or consider_with_caution statuses accordingly. Graph edges encode the drug-class relationship while PostgreSQL rules encode executable washout logic. Vector retrieval alone often retrieves ARNI benefits and ACE inhibitor warnings in separate chunks; graph and rule layers exist to enforce such compositional constraints.

GDMT benefits accrue when agents are uptitrated toward target doses tolerated by the patient, not merely initiated at starting dose [6]. Labels specify starting doses, doubling intervals, and maximum recommended doses; guidelines emphasize reassessment of blood pressure, heart rate, potassium, and renal function after each change. HF-CDSS dose-rule catalogs encode these schedules as structured objects queried by the dosing engine, separate from narrative retrieval. Monitoring intervals, such as potassium within one week of MRA initiation, appear as monitoring relations in the graph and as caution prompts when recent labs are stale or missing. Titration support requires stateful patient profiles (current dose step, date of last change) rather than stateless question answering, which explains why GDMT gap detection, constraint checking, and dose calculation are deterministic services while GraphRAG supplies explanatory evidence.

## 2.8 Implementation Technologies Used in This Thesis

The HF-CDSS prototype combines several technologies, each chosen for a clear role in the architecture.

PostgreSQL is a relational database that stores governed clinical rules, user accounts, audit logs, and approval workflow state. JSONB columns hold structured dose schedules and renal adjustment objects that can be queried at runtime without a schema migration for every new agent variant. Relational storage suits transactional rule approval and version history.

Neo4j is a graph database queried with Cypher for neighborhood expansion and typed fact retrieval. Property graph models align naturally with drug-interaction-disease relations. Batch import from JSONL relationship files produced by the ingestion pipeline populates the clinical knowledge graph.

ChromaDB is a vector store that persists embedding vectors over clinical text chunks with metadata filters. It supports hybrid dense retrieval integrated with Python ingestion jobs. Chunks carry drug and section metadata so queries can be scoped to relevant corpora subsets.

FastAPI is a Python web framework that provides async HTTP APIs with automatic request validation (via Pydantic) and OpenAPI documentation. Async handlers allow concurrent retrieval over ChromaDB, BM25, Neo4j, and local language-model inference without blocking during I/O-bound operations. Server-Sent Events (SSE) stream typed pipeline events so physicians see structured recommendations before narrative explanation completes.

React is a JavaScript library for building the clinician dashboard user interface. Component-based design supports bilingual strings, conversation history, and progressive disclosure of draft parsing, safety checks, verification, and answer tokens. The ClinicalPanel presents structured recommendation cards decoupled from language-model prose, implementing the Five Rights principle of delivering information in the right format.

Redis caches approved rule snapshots, conversation drafts, and repeated language-model outputs, reducing database and inference load during multi-turn chat. Docker Compose orchestrates backend, databases, vector store, graph database, and optional GPU-enabled Ollama containers for reproducible local deployment. Together, these components form a modular stack: PostgreSQL and Neo4j for structured knowledge, ChromaDB for textual evidence, FastAPI for orchestration, React for presentation, and Ollama for on-premises language-model inference.

## 2.9 Techniques Adopted in This Thesis

The HF-CDSS system selectively implements the techniques surveyed above rather than adopting every method described in the research literature. HyDE expands short physician queries via a small language model before BGE-M3 embedding, improving recall when questions are terse but never replacing corroborating retrieved chunks and graph facts [14]. BM25 sparse retrieval ensures brand names, numeric cutoffs, and phrases like "36 hours" appear in candidate lists even when dense embeddings underweight them. BGE-M3 provides a single embedding model for both ingestion section filtering and query-time dense retrieval. Reciprocal Rank Fusion with k = 60 merges Chroma dense search, BM25, and Neo4j-derived evidence channels without normalizing incompatible score scales.

The three-tier section filter applies keyword matching, then embedding similarity, then borderline LLM review at ingestion, minimizing expensive model calls while preserving clinical content. Hybrid regex and lexicon intake extracts vitals, labs, and common medications reliably at query time; LLM extraction fills gaps when confidence is low, with merge policy preferring measured numeric values over inferred ones. The GDMT rule engine implements executable policies for ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, beta blocker, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor gaps and safety checks in PostgreSQL. Verification agents enforce fail-closed behavior on hard blocks, check that evidence citations reference retrieved chunk IDs, and flag missing critical patient fields before final answer delivery.

Neo4j local graph search supplies entity-centric neighborhood facts for GraphRAG context. SSE streaming implements progressive disclosure aligned with clinician cognitive load. Role-based access control separates bedside clinicians from clinical leads who approve rules. Optional query decomposition and optional API-based reranking can sharpen retrieval when enabled in configuration. Techniques deliberately not adopted as primary decision mechanisms include end-to-end neural prescribing, unconstrained cloud-only language models, and pure vector RAG without graph or rule layers, each rejected for insufficient audibility in heart-failure medication safety.

At query time, physician chat triggers hybrid intake, deterministic GDMT and constraint reasoning, parallel GraphRAG retrieval (HyDE, dense search, BM25, Neo4j facts, RRF fusion, optional rerank), multi-agent verification, and SSE-delivered bilingual explanation. This combines the audibility of rule-based CDSS with the lexical flexibility of modern retrieval and generation.

## 2.10 Chapter Summary

This chapter surveyed the theoretical foundations underlying the HF-CDSS thesis. Clinical decision support systems were defined as workflow-aware tools that augment rather than replace clinician judgment, with historical development from MYCIN to modern hybrid systems illustrating recurring needs for transparency, specificity, and timely delivery [15]-[17]. Osheroff's Five Rights and quantitative alert metrics provided evaluation lenses; heart-failure-specific challenges motivated combining rules, graphs, and retrieval rather than any single paradigm.

Knowledge graphs were presented as explicit networks of entities and relations supporting entity linking, multi-hop traversal, and relational fidelity beyond flat documents [7], [8]. Retrieval-augmented generation was developed from dense embeddings and sparse BM25 through hybrid fusion, chunking, and vector indexing, with healthcare failure modes motivating deterministic safety layers [12]. Large language models were grounded in Transformer architecture and scaling milestones, with prompting, hallucination risks, and local inference completing the foundation [18], [19]. GraphRAG was introduced as graph-augmented retrieval fused with text evidence, including RRF, HyDE, and local graph search rationale [13], [14].

Medical knowledge construction covered ingestion sources, extraction and linking pipelines, multi-store synchronization, and the three-tier section filter. Heart failure clinical foundations summarized HFrEF phenotype, GDMT pillars, key labs and vitals, ACE inhibitor to ARNI washout, and titration monitoring as minimal cardiology for implementation [2], [3], [6]. Implementation technologies were explained in plain terms. Adopted techniques were mapped to concrete system roles.

The through-line is hybridism: no single technique, whether rules, graphs, vectors, or language models, is sufficient alone for heart-failure medication safety at conversational interfaces. Deterministic modules supply auditable decisions; retrieval supplies citations; graphs supply relational constraints; language models supply flexible bilingual explanation under verification and governance. Chapter 3 translates these foundations into concrete requirements, module boundaries, database schemas, and API contracts for the HF-CDSS implementation.
