# CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSION

## 6.1 Summary of Contributions

This thesis researched, designed, implemented, and evaluated a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) specialized for heart failure. The system integrates a medical Knowledge Graph, hybrid GraphRAG retrieval, deterministic clinical rule engines, verification agents, and locally hosted large language models to deliver personalized, evidence-grounded treatment recommendations while keeping clinicians in the decision loop. Rather than treating the LLM as an autonomous prescriber, the architecture assigns it roles where probabilistic language generation adds value—intake fallback, borderline section review, evidence-grounded explanation—while reserving authoritative status for PostgreSQL-backed constraint, interaction, dose, and GDMT catalogs evaluated by deterministic Python services.

The work was motivated by a persistent clinical gap: heart failure with reduced ejection fraction has strong evidence for four-drug GDMT, yet real-world implementation rates remain near 1–2% at target doses. Simultaneously, generative AI chat tools promise natural language interaction but lack governed safety catalogs suitable for pharmacotherapy. The thesis hypothesis—that hybrid coupling resolves this tension—was operationalized as implementable software with measurable criteria (Section 1.5) rather than as a conceptual position paper alone. Success required not only accurate recommendations but also sub-10-second median latency, bilingual UI, automated knowledge pipeline output, and fail-closed contraindication handling—criteria reported against in Chapter 5 and reflected upon below.

Architecturally, the work proposes a three-tier design spanning presentation, application, and data layers, combined with GraphRAG for heart-failure–specialized support. The presentation layer delivers a React 18 + Vite doctor dashboard with SSE streaming chat, the `ClinicalPanel` recommendation card grid, and bilingual language toggling that regenerates simplified plain-language labels without re-running clinical inference. The application layer implements FastAPI async orchestration across hybrid clinical intake, reasoning, GraphRAG, verification, dose calculation, and card summarization modules. The data layer persists governance catalogs in PostgreSQL, caches session and LLM responses in Redis, stores BGE-M3 embeddings in ChromaDB, maintains entity–relation structure in Neo4j, and versions raw and processed pipeline artifacts in S3-compatible object storage (LocalStack in development). This design enables efficient retrieval of medical knowledge from both graph and vector indexes, generation of evidence-cited recommendations, and real-time incremental feedback through Server-Sent Events.

On the knowledge engineering side, the thesis contributes an automated ingestion pipeline that acquires FDA Structured Product Labels via asynchronous HTTP from DailyMed, stages raw XML/PDF/HTML in a raw S3 bucket, parses and section-identifies clinical content, and applies a cost-aware three-tier section filter: keyword matching for high-precision headings, BGE-M3 cosine similarity with keep threshold ≥ 0.52, borderline LLM review for scores in [0.40, 0.52), and automatic drop below 0.40. Filtered text undergoes sentence-aware chunking with approximately 512-token windows and overlap, followed by named entity recognition, relation and claim extraction, and LLM structured rule extraction constrained by JSON schema and Pydantic models. Extracted artifacts are classified into safety tiers (`hard_block`, `usable_rules`, `needs_condition_refinement`) and action types (`avoid`, `consider_with_caution`, `consider`, `continue`) before sync to PostgreSQL governance catalogs and parallel indexing into ChromaDB and Neo4j. Evaluation demonstrated that this cascade retained 95.0% of sections while invoking borderline LLM review on only 6.6% of inputs—a 93.4% reduction relative to naive per-section LLM review. The pipeline processed 127 registered drugs in the manifest, integrated 60 for full evaluation extraction yielding 4,136 label sections, and populated catalogs with 6,032 constraint rules and 1,096 interaction rules—demonstrating scale beyond toy examples while acknowledging dose rule completion and formulary expansion as ongoing work.

On the query-time reasoning side, the thesis contributes a hybrid clinical intake pipeline combining regular-expression extraction of numeric vitals and laboratories, lexicon-based medication and condition detection with negation handling, BGE-M3 semantic matching above a 0.52 threshold, and selective LLM extraction when regex confidence is insufficient, merging streams with preference for measured values. Missing eGFR may be derived deterministically from creatinine, age, and sex. The reasoning engine evaluates GDMT coverage per drug class, applies PostgreSQL constraint and interaction rules, and computes dosing from dose-rule catalogs independently of LLM output. In parallel, a hybrid GraphRAG service performs HyDE query expansion, dense ChromaDB retrieval, BM25 sparse retrieval, Neo4j neighborhood traversal, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion with optional semantic reranking to supply citation-ready evidence to the explanation layer without overriding structured statuses. End-to-end orchestration via SSE streams structured events in safety-first order: `draft_ready`, `recommendation_ready`, `verification_ready`, then `answer_delta` tokens—implementing Osheroff's Five Rights timing principle in software protocol rather than policy document alone.

On safety and explainability, the thesis contributes a deliberate separation between deterministic GDMT/safety classification and LLM-generated narrative. Hard blocks enforce fail-closed contraindications; verification agents audit recommendations for evidence retrieval consistency and guideline alignment before SSE completion; and the card summarizer maps structured fields to Vietnamese and English plain-language labels deterministically. Governance catalogs expose constraint rules, dose rules, GDMT policies, and interaction rules for admin review, including rules flagged `needs_condition_refinement` rather than silently discarding imperfect extractions. Alert optimization reduced alerts per patient from 8.2 to 4.3 while preserving hard-block visibility—demonstrating that safety UX tuning and fail-closed enforcement are compatible when deduplication targets redundant cautions rather than absolute contraindications.

Experimentally, treatment recommendation accuracy reached 94.0% (95% CI [89.2%, 98.8%]) on 50 cardiologist-reviewed cases, exceeding the 90% target. Average end-to-end response time was 8.1 seconds (P50 7.4 seconds), meeting the under-10-second requirement on median despite P95 12.6 seconds. Safety-issue sensitivity was 92.5%; interaction detection achieved F1 97.1%; and user satisfaction among 25 surveyed cardiologists averaged 4.22 out of 5.0, with clinical usefulness rated highest at 4.5. Alert optimization reduced alerts per patient from 8.2 to 4.3 while all structured safety test scenarios passed. Knowledge construction achieved 94.2% label extraction success, 95.0% section retention, and 45-second average per-drug processing—establishing that offline indexing cost is manageable for periodic catalog refresh cycles.

Scientifically, the work contributes a GraphRAG-based method for heart-failure CDSS that fuses graph and vector retrieval with deterministic rule engines; an automated multi-source pipeline for building governed medical knowledge bases from FDA labels and guidelines; and a cost-aware three-tier section filter and hybrid intake architecture that reserve LLM capacity for uncertain cases. Practically, the system supports guideline-aligned GDMT decisions, reduces prescribing risk through tiered safety alerts and verification, and saves clinicians time searching drug and guideline information—benefits especially relevant where specialist capacity is limited and bilingual communication is required.

Each contribution area warrants elaboration because the thesis integrates them into a single deployable stack rather than treating them as isolated papers. The **GraphRAG method** is not generic RAG with a graph database attached: Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges ChromaDB dense rankings, BM25 sparse rankings, and graph-derived chunk boosts; HyDE expansion addresses query-document vocabulary mismatch; Neo4j neighborhood retrieval supplies multi-hop facts for verification agents. Interaction F1 of 97.1% and ACEi/ARB/ARNI F1 of 95.5% provide empirical anchors that hybrid retrieval supports high-stakes drug-class reasoning when paired with PostgreSQL rules—not that GraphRAG alone replaces those rules.

The **automated knowledge pipeline** transforms FDA SPL XML and eight HF guidelines into 6,032 constraint rules, 1,096 interaction rules, and vector/graph indexes without manual transcription of each label section. The 94.2% extraction success rate and 45-second per-drug average demonstrate batch operability. The three-tier section filter is a reusable ingestion pattern: keyword certainty, embedding similarity, LLM only in the borderline band—retaining 95.0% of sections with 354 LLM calls instead of 5,381. Pipeline outputs land in governable PostgreSQL rows with draft/approved/retired status, not opaque model weights—clinical_leads retain veto power over automation.

The **hybrid intake and reasoning stack** converts chat text into typed profiles with mean 1.2-second extraction, evaluates GDMT pillars deterministically, and streams bilingual cards before LLM narrative completes. Mean 8.1-second total latency with P50 7.4 seconds meets median workflow targets on RTX 3080 hardware with local Ollama—demonstrating that on-premise deployment is feasible without cloud API dependency for core inference.

The **safety and verification layer** implements fail-closed hard_block enforcement (667 rules at ingestion classification), four-for-four safety scenario passes, and alert burden reduction from 8.2 to 4.3 per patient. Safety sensitivity 92.5% with 9.2% alert false positive rate reflects a caution-biased operating point appropriate to pharmacotherapy CDSS.

The **bilingual clinical interface** achieves 4.22/5 satisfaction with language switching under 2 seconds and deterministic card summarization in Vietnamese and English—addressing a gap in English-only comparators (Section 5.5.1).

### 6.1.3. Comparison with Initial Objectives (Chapter 1)

Chapter 1 listed four technical stages and enumerated techniques by name (BGE-M3, RRF, HyDE, JSON schema extraction, etc.). The implemented system executes each stage: knowledge engineering produced 4,136 label sections and 6,032 constraints; hybrid clinical reasoning achieved 94.0% accuracy; verification and explanation delivered bilingual SSE streaming with 4.22/5 satisfaction; evaluation measured all Section 1.5 criteria with partial exception for dose rule catalog completeness. The initial claim that "the LLM is an interface and explanation layer, not the sole source of clinical truth" is substantiated by safety test design requiring pass on structured JSON irrespective of LLM wording, and by card summarizer determinism decoupled from Qwen2.5-7B narrative generation.

Where initial ambitions exceeded delivered scope—127 versus 60 fully integrated drugs, dose rules "in progress," no FHIR—the limitations are documented transparently in Sections 6.3 and 5.8 rather than conflated with core hybrid architecture validation. The thesis therefore meets its stated purpose to **design, implement, and evaluate** a heart failure CDSS, with evaluation bounded to supervised vignette and survey methodology rather than outcome trials.

The contributions may be classified along four axes useful for readers assessing transferability to other domains. **Methodological contributions** include the hybrid LLM gating pattern (deterministic or embedding first, generative only on uncertainty), RRF fusion across dense, sparse, and graph retrievers, and SSE event ordering that prioritizes structured safety payloads before narrative tokens. **Engineering contributions** include the modular monolith FastAPI backend, Docker Compose deployment profile with on-premise Ollama inference, JSONL-to-PostgreSQL governance sync with draft/approved/retired lifecycles, and bilingual deterministic card summarization decoupled from LLM generation. **Empirical contributions** include the integrated evaluation protocol reporting 94.0% accuracy, 8.1s mean latency, 95.0% section retention at 6.6% borderline LLM cost, and cardiologist satisfaction 4.22/5 on the evaluated stack. **Clinical-process contributions** include GDMT four-pillar gap visualization, interaction checking with 97.1% F1, dose titration plans from JSONB rules, and fail-closed hard_block enforcement validated on four high-risk safety scenarios.

Readers building diabetes or anticoagulation CDSS can transfer the **governance pipeline pattern** and **SSE dual-channel protocol** relatively directly, while replacing GDMT pillar policies and drug-class lexicons. Readers studying LLM safety in medicine can treat HF-CDSS as a case study in **layered enforcement**: deterministic rules, retrieval validation, verification agents, and human review—each layer compensates for weaknesses in adjacent layers, as error analysis (Section 5.6) demonstrates when intake omits medications but hard_block rules still protect against known co-prescription errors when drugs are detected.

## 6.2 Reflection on Research Questions

Chapter 1 posed a central research problem: how to combine structured medical knowledge, deterministic safety logic, and LLM-mediated interaction so that heart-failure decision support remains accurate, explainable, and clinically usable. The thesis statement claimed that a hybrid CDSS coupling deterministic GDMT and safety rules with Knowledge Graph–augmented retrieval and LLM explanation can deliver accurate, timely, and bilingual support while keeping clinicians in the loop. The evaluation results support affirmative answers to the implicit research questions, with nuance documented in Section 5.8 threats to validity.

**Can hybrid architecture achieve guideline-level recommendation accuracy without delegating safety to the LLM?** The 94.0% accuracy on structured recommendation objects, exceeding the 90% success criterion, supports a qualified yes. Accuracy was measured on deterministic outputs, not LLM prose; MRA recall (89.3%) and intake-dependent errors (Section 5.6) indicate remaining gaps where laboratory completeness and local drug nomenclature matter. Hard_block rules and verification agents ensure contraindications encoded in catalogs are not silently overridden—100% pass on four curated safety scenarios confirms the safety delegation question is answered more strongly than the GDMT optimization question. Specificity at 95.2% exceeding sensitivity at 92.5% further indicates the system errs toward caution—clinically appropriate for pharmacotherapy CDSS where false approval of contraindicated therapy harms more than false warnings.

**Can cost-aware LLM gating make automated knowledge construction operationally viable?** The three-tier section filter retaining 95.0% of sections with 96.6% LLM call avoidance, and hybrid intake completing in mean 1.2 seconds, support yes for the evaluated document corpus and hardware profile. Guideline borderline rates (12.5%) higher than label rates (4.8%) suggest ongoing tuning as new publisher formats appear. Ingestion at 45 seconds per drug is batch-viable for quarterly label updates but not real-time discovery. The 93.4% LLM call reduction at ingestion (354 versus 5,381 hypothetical full reviews) is the operational headline: hospitals can refresh knowledge bases on commodity GPU hardware without cloud LLM budgets proportional to document volume.

**Does GraphRAG hybrid retrieval improve explainability and trust relative to vector-only or LLM-only approaches?** Direct A/B comparison was not executed, but qualitative ablation (Section 5.3.2.1) and high interaction F1 (97.1%) suggest RRF fusion and graph neighborhoods contribute to evidence-rich explanation without replacing deterministic rules. Clinician satisfaction on recommendation accuracy perception (4.1/5) and clinical usefulness (4.5/5) indicates process value beyond raw accuracy—clinicians appear to trust structured cards plus cited narrative more than either layer alone would provide. Evidence agent design—failing verification when zero chunks retrieve—creates engineering pressure to maintain ingestion coverage, linking offline pipeline metrics (95.0% retention) to online trust metrics.

**Is sub-10-second interactive latency achievable with local LLM inference on modest hospital hardware?** Mean 8.1 seconds and P50 7.4 seconds meet the median target; P95 12.6 seconds does not meet a strict P95 < 10 seconds interpretation of NFR-1. SSE streaming mitigates perceived delay. LLM answer generation (mean 3.5s) remains the optimization bottleneck. Reasoning at 2.1s and GraphRAG at 0.8s demonstrate that deterministic and retrieval stages are not the primary scaling concern on evaluated hardware—generative explanation is.

**Can bilingual Vietnamese–English support coexist with conversation continuity and deterministic safety display?** Language switching under 2 seconds with zero data loss, default Vietnamese locale in `LanguageProvider`, and deterministic card summarizer maps support yes for the UI layer. Intake recall on Vietnamese brand names remains incomplete—a bilingual interface does not by itself solve localized nomenclature in extraction lexicons. Future work must extend bilingualism from presentation (solved) to intake and acquisition (partially open).

These answers collectively validate the thesis statement within evaluated bounds: hybrid CDSS for heart failure is feasible, accurate enough for supervised clinical use, and differentiated from English-only, non-chat, or LLM-only alternatives—while not yet proven as an outcomes-improving intervention.

The research questions interconnect: accuracy without LLM safety delegation requires deterministic catalogs; cost-aware gating makes both ingestion and intake scalable; GraphRAG and verification jointly support explainability; latency targets constrain model size and streaming design; bilingual support splits into intake lexicon work versus presentation localization. Weakness in any one dimension—Vietnamese drug names, P95 latency, dose rule incompleteness—limits overall deployment readiness without invalidating the hybrid pattern itself. Future work should therefore extend the pattern's components rather than replacing hybrid architecture with monolithic LLM end-to-end models that sacrifice auditability.

## 6.3 Limitations

Several limitations bound the conclusions that can be drawn from this work and the readiness of the system for unsupervised clinical deployment. Drug coverage remains incomplete: although 127 drugs appear in the sources registry, accuracy evaluation focused on 60 fully integrated agents, and many medications commonly prescribed in Vietnam—including local brands identified only in Vietnamese—are not yet reliably recognized by intake lexicons. This gap directly affects medication recall and interaction checking for real-world Vietnamese formularies until synonym catalogs are integrated as described in Chapter 5. Expanding from 60 to 127 integrated agents is primarily an engineering and governance workload—pipeline re-runs, clinical_lead review of new interaction pairs—not a fundamental architecture change; the bottleneck is lexical coverage and approval throughput, not reasoning algorithm limits.

Patient data entry depends on free-text chat rather than HL7/FHIR feeds from hospital information systems. Manual entry increases omission rates for creatinine, eGFR, and potassium, propagating into derived eGFR estimates, renal contraindication alerts with 12.6% false positives, and slightly reduced MRA recall (89.3%). Without structured interoperability, the CDSS cannot automatically synchronize with authoritative laboratory sources or export recommendations into the EHR record in standard formats. Chat-based intake was chosen for thesis scope and rapid iteration—it is a **limitation**, not a design ideal for production ward integration.

Although GraphRAG grounding and verification agents reduce hallucination risk relative to standalone LLM chatbots, generative narrative may still contain incorrect statements or omit nuance present only in unraveled guideline prose. The 94.0% accuracy metric applies to structured recommendation objects reviewed by cardiologists, not to every token of LLM answer text; human-in-the-loop review therefore remains necessary before recommendations inform prescribing or titration decisions. Teaching materials for pilot deployment should train clinicians to read GDMT cards and verification badges before acting on chat paragraphs—a workflow habit reinforced by SSE ordering but not guaranteed by software alone.

The evaluation design was retrospective on curated vignettes rather than prospective interventional study. Usability survey results (4.22/5 overall) and latency measurements (8.1s mean) reflect controlled tasks and a specific hardware stack (RTX 3080, 32 GB RAM); generalization to lower-resource settings or high-concurrency hospital deployment requires further validation. Finally, access is web-based through the doctor dashboard without a native mobile client, limiting bedside workflows where clinicians prefer handheld devices.

Accepting these limitations does not diminish the thesis contribution—it defines the **boundary conditions** under which reported metrics apply. Claims of 94.0% accuracy attach to cardiologist-reviewed structured outputs on 60 integrated drugs with complete laboratory vignettes, not to all possible HF patients or all Vietnamese formulary agents. Claims of 8.1s mean latency attach to RTX 3080 GPU-accelerated Ollama on 16-core hardware, not to CPU-only ward terminals. Claims of 4.22/5 satisfaction attach to 25 cardiologists after guided tasks, not longitudinal real-world adoption. Precision in claim scope is as important as precision in GDMT dosing.

### 6.3.1. Limitations by System Layer

Limitations distribute unevenly across layers, guiding prioritized remediation. The **knowledge layer** retains 35.2% of extracted rules in `needs_condition_refinement` status, dose rules remain incomplete, and 5.0% of sections are dropped at ingestion—each constraining catalog completeness. The **reasoning layer** depends on intake quality; derived eGFR and ambiguous creatinine units propagate errors into renal alerts. The **explanation layer** inherits LLM stochasticity despite verification; bilingual card text is deterministic but chat narrative is not. The **deployment layer** assumes Docker Compose single-host topology without multi-tenant isolation or formal HIPAA certification processes. The **evaluation layer** uses n=50 vignettes and n=25 survey respondents—adequate for thesis demonstration, insufficient for regulatory submission or definitive outcome claims.

Additional limitations merit explicit mention. **Polypharmacy beyond GDMT** is partially modeled: interaction rules cover registered pairs, but comprehensive geriatric polypharmacy in real Vietnamese practice includes herbals, OTC agents, and non-cardiovascular drugs not in the 127-drug manifest. **Comorbidity reasoning** beyond heart failure—advanced CKD on dialysis, active malignancy, pregnancy—is not fully encoded in constraint catalogs focused on GDMT initiation and titration. **Guideline versioning** is snapshot-based: evaluation used catalogs synchronized at a point in time; ESC or AHA updates require pipeline re-sync and clinical_lead re-approval rather than automatic propagation. **Equity and bias** were not formally assessed: training sources are US/EU-centric labels and international guidelines; applicability to resource-limited settings without GPU hardware is unverified beyond the stated RTX 3080 profile. **Security evaluation** stopped at design-level threat modeling (Chapter 3.7.4) without penetration testing or formal privacy impact assessment.

**Scalability limits** also deserve mention. The evaluation targeted 50 concurrent users (NFR-1) but did not load-test at that concurrency with GPU-saturated Ollama inference; production multi-user deployment may require request queuing, model replication, or answer-length caps. PostgreSQL constraint matching over thousands of rules completed in 2.1s mean for single-user evaluation; index tuning and rule partitioning by drug class may be necessary at higher catalog sizes. ChromaDB and Neo4j co-residence on single-host Compose simplifies thesis reproducibility but is not a high-availability architecture—hospital IT may require managed database services and backup SLAs beyond `pg_dump` scripts in Section 4.7.2.

**Regulatory pathway** is unaddressed: the thesis does not claim FDA Software as a Medical Device clearance, CE marking, or Vietnamese medical device registration. Supervised pilot use in research settings differs materially from marketed clinical product claims. Future regulatory work would need risk classification analysis, clinical evaluation reports, and post-market surveillance plans aligned with local authority guidance—outside thesis scope but mandatory before commercial deployment.

## 6.4 Future Work

Future work proceeds along parallel tracks addressing the limitations above while extending the hybrid architecture to broader clinical and research contexts.

### 6.4.3. Vietnamese Localization Depth

Vietnamese support in the current system spans UI chrome, deterministic card summarizer strings, intake diacritic normalization, and default `LanguageProvider` locale (`vi`). It does **not** yet span full medication nomenclature alignment, localized guideline ingestion, or LLM fine-tuning on Vietnamese clinical prose. Future localization should proceed in layers mirroring the architecture: first expand acquisition and intake lexicons with Ministry of Health and hospital formulary synonyms (targeting the dominant error class in Section 5.6); second ingest Vietnamese-translated or locally authored HF guidelines where ESC English PDFs are insufficient for patient-facing counseling; third evaluate whether Qwen2.5-7B zero-shot Vietnamese narrative quality suffices or requires domain fine-tuning—without moving safety classification into the fine-tuned model. Localization must not become "translate the LLM only" while leaving drug identifiers English-centric; that pattern would widen the gap between chat fluency and structured recommendation accuracy.

### 6.4.4. Interoperability and EHR Integration

FHIR integration represents the highest-impact near-term engineering effort because it attacks intake omission root causes rather than symptoms. A minimal viable integration consumes `Patient` (age, sex), `Observation` (creatinine, potassium, eGFR, weight), and `MedicationStatement` (active GDMT agents) via SMART-on-FHIR or hospital-specific APIs, populating `PatientProfile` fields before chat merge. Export should write `DocumentReference` or `CarePlan` artifacts containing structured recommendation JSON plus verification verdict for EHR audit—not LLM prose alone. Bidirectional sync raises consent, authentication, and break-glass access issues beyond current JWT chat auth; production pilots require institutional IRB and IT security review. Three-month scope (Section 5.6.2 improvement plan) is realistic for read-only laboratory and medication import; write-back is medium horizon.

Expanding the drug database with more than one hundred agents commonly used for heart failure in Vietnam is an immediate priority. This effort should integrate Vietnamese pharmaceutical catalogs and synonym resources so that local brand and generic names map to the same RxNorm-aligned identifiers used in constraint and interaction rules, closing the most frequent intake error class identified in evaluation. Complementing lexical expansion, named-entity recognition and semantic intake matchers should be fine-tuned or lexicon-augmented for Vietnamese clinical text, preserving the hybrid pattern of regex-first extraction with LLM fallback rather than replacing deterministic parsers entirely.

FHIR and HL7 integration should connect the CDSS to hospital HIS and EHR systems so that Patient, Observation, MedicationStatement, and Condition resources populate the structured profile automatically and recommendations can be exported as DocumentReference or CarePlan artifacts for audit. A focused integration effort of roughly three months with backend and interoperability specialists is a realistic initial scope, targeting laboratory observations (creatinine, potassium, eGFR), active medications, and demographics sufficient for GDMT and renal safety evaluation without requiring full note ingestion on day one.

In the medium term, the same modular monolith architecture can extend to adjacent domains where heart failure comorbidity is common—diabetes management with glucose and insulin dosing, chronic kidney disease with renal dose adjustment and dialysis considerations, and anticoagulation with DOAC or warfarin management and bleeding-risk assessment—reusing GraphRAG infrastructure, governance catalog patterns, and verification agent frameworks. A React Native mobile client with offline caching of recent recommendations and push notifications for critical interaction alerts would broaden access in connectivity-limited wards. A multi-patient clinical dashboard aggregating GDMT gap counts, alert burdens, and follow-up laboratories could support heart-failure clinic population management.

Domain extension should reuse **catalog schema and pipeline mechanics** while replacing clinical content: GDMT policies become disease-specific protocol objects; constraint rules encode new drug classes; intake lexicons expand to new medication lists. GraphRAG collections may partition by `metadata.disease` to prevent diabetes chunks from ranking highly in HF queries. Verification agent templates generalize with domain-specific tool hooks. This reuse strategy avoids rebuilding SSE protocols, governance lifecycles, and card summarizer patterns— amortizing thesis engineering investment across comorbidity modules common in HF patients.

Longer-term research directions include fine-tuning a clinical-domain LLM from a base model such as Qwen2.5-7B on de-identified bilingual notes, labeled rule extractions, and interaction pairs, with expected gains in Vietnamese terminology handling, borderline intake resolution, and extraction quality for `needs_condition_refinement` rules—while maintaining deterministic enforcement for `hard_block` tiers regardless of model confidence. Federated learning across hospitals could improve intake and retrieval models without centralizing protected health information. Real-time clinical trial matching based on structured eligibility criteria represents another GraphRAG application of the Neo4j entity layer.

Rigorous follow-up studies should evaluate the CDSS in controlled hospital settings, for example through a randomized trial of approximately two hundred heart-failure patients with GDMT optimization as the primary outcome and thirty-day readmission as a secondary outcome. Production A/B experiments comparing hybrid GraphRAG with classical vector-only RAG should quantify precision, recall, latency, and clinician satisfaction differences attributable specifically to RRF fusion and graph traversal—addressing the qualitative ablation gap noted in Section 5.8. Explainability research—knowledge-graph path visualization, retrieved evidence highlighting, and explicit citation anchors in SSE answer streams—would further increase clinical trust beyond accuracy metrics alone.

### 6.4.2. Research Agenda Summary

Future work clusters into three horizons without inventing new performance targets beyond those already measured. **Near horizon (0–6 months):** Vietnamese drug synonym integration; FHIR Observation and MedicationStatement ingestion; dose rule catalog completion; expanded safety regression suite beyond four scenarios; P95 latency reduction via answer streaming optimizations and verification caching. **Medium horizon (6–18 months):** React Native mobile client; drug interaction API for third-party systems; multi-patient clinic dashboard; comorbidity module prototypes (CKD stage 4–5, diabetes); production A/B of vector-only versus hybrid GraphRAG with controlled latency and satisfaction measurement on identical vignettes. **Long horizon (18+ months):** bilingual LLM fine-tuning on de-identified notes; federated learning for intake matchers; randomized outcome trial (~200 patients, GDMT optimization primary endpoint, thirty-day readmission secondary); graph-path explainability UI with citation anchors in SSE streams.

Each horizon preserves the architectural non-negotiable: **hard_block rules and deterministic GDMT statuses remain authoritative** regardless of model improvements—a design commitment that should not be traded for marginal accuracy gains on narrative fluency.

### 6.4.5. Collaboration and Stakeholder Requirements

Sustained evolution of HF-CDSS requires ongoing collaboration between software engineers, clinical leads, and hospital IT—not a one-time thesis delivery. **Clinical leads** must review `needs_condition_refinement` rules, approve or retire constraint changes after label updates, and adjudicate false positive alert reports from ward pilots. **Engineers** must re-run ingestion on schedule, monitor borderline LLM counts, tune section filter thresholds when new SPL formats appear, and maintain Ollama model versions with regression tests on the 50-case suite. **Hospital IT** must provide FHIR endpoints, TLS certificates, backup policies, and GPU capacity planning for concurrent users. **Researchers** should lead outcome studies and A/B tests rather than expanding feature scope without evidence. The thesis architecture supports this division of labor through admin APIs, governance lifecycles, and separated deterministic versus generative components—each stakeholder can own a layer without understanding every module.

Without institutional clinical governance, even 94.0% accurate rule catalogs drift stale as guidelines change; the pipeline exists precisely to make refresh cycles repeatable, not to eliminate human oversight.

## 6.5 Implications for CDSS Research and Practice

The thesis findings carry implications beyond the specific HF-CDSS implementation, contributing to ongoing debates about how large language models should enter regulated clinical workflows. First, the 94.0% accuracy on structured outputs versus the irreducible hallucination risk in generative narrative reinforces a emerging consensus: LLM deployment in pharmacotherapy should standardize on dual-channel delivery—machine-readable decision artifacts with deterministic provenance, plus optional natural-language explanation—rather than single-channel chat transcripts that merge fact and rhetoric. The SSE event ordering (`recommendation_ready` and `verification_ready` before `answer_delta`) is a concrete protocol pattern other CDSS builders can adopt without replicating heart-failure-specific rules.

Second, the three-tier section filter and hybrid intake demonstrate that **LLM cost and safety trade-offs are architectural**, not merely model-selection, problems. Gating functions—embedding thresholds, keyword catchments, merge policies preferring measured values—reduce LLM exposure by 93.4% at ingestion and keep intake near 1.2s mean at query time. Research programs pursuing "LLM-first CDSS" without analogous gating should report comparable cost and latency baselines before claims of clinical readiness.

Third, governance catalogs with draft/approved/retired lifecycles address a chronic failure mode of rule-based systems: knowledge staleness. Automated extraction producing 53.9% immediately usable rules and 35.2% refinement candidates creates a sustainable update path when FDA labels and ESC guidelines revise—operators re-run `--from-step kg_base`, clinical_leads approve diffs, and runtime loaders refresh without application redeployment. This closes the loop between knowledge engineering (Chapter 4) and query-time reasoning (Chapter 3) that static guideline PDFs cannot offer.

Fourth, bilingual deterministic simplification decouples **clinical truth from presentation locale**—a requirement underestimated in English-centric CDSS literature. The under-2-second language switch with zero data loss proves localization can be a presentation-layer concern when structured statuses remain locale-invariant; intake lexicon localization remains separate work, as Vietnamese drug errors attest.

Practitioners evaluating similar systems should demand: structured recommendation JSON inspectable independent of chat prose; hard-block rules non-overridable by LLM layers; retrieval citations validated against fetched chunks; and latency decompositions isolating generative bottlenecks. HF-CDSS meets these demands on evaluated hardware; adopters should verify on their infrastructure before clinical piloting.

### 6.5.1. Ethical and Responsible AI Considerations

Clinical decision support carries ethical obligations beyond accuracy percentages. **Transparency:** HF-CDSS exposes structured statuses, verification verdicts, and retrieved evidence chunks rather than black-box scores—clinicians can inspect why MRA received `consider_with_caution`. **Autonomy:** Recommendations are advisory; the interface does not auto-prescribe or write orders. **Safety:** Fail-closed hard blocks prioritize patient harm prevention over convenience; 92.5% sensitivity with explicit safety scenario testing reflects this ordering. **Fairness:** Evaluation did not stratify by sex, age, or comorbidity subgroups; future work should audit whether MRA recall disparities correlate with demographic patterns in vignettes. **Environmental cost:** Local GPU inference consumes electricity; three-tier LLM gating reduces ingestion compute versus naive full-corpus review—a sustainability side benefit of hybrid design. **Dual use:** The same pipeline could theoretically extract rules for non-HF domains; governance workflows should prevent unsupervised deployment in domains lacking clinical_lead oversight.

Responsible deployment means refusing to market the system as "AI doctor" or equivalent—language that would misrepresent 94.0% structured accuracy and LLM narrative risk. The thesis terminology "clinician-supervised assistant" is intentional.

## 6.6 Deployment Readiness Assessment

Deployment readiness is partial—appropriate for supervised pilot in research-affiliated clinics, not for unsupervised clinical use or regulatory clearance without further evidence. Readiness strengths include: Docker Compose reproducibility; health endpoints for dependency monitoring; fail-closed safety on four high-risk scenarios; cardiologist satisfaction 4.22/5; and on-premise inference avoiding cloud PHI egress. Readiness gaps include: incomplete dose rule catalog; 60 versus 127 drug integration; absent FHIR connectivity; n=50 accuracy cohort; no prospective outcome data; and web-only access without mobile client.

A pragmatic deployment roadmap phases risk: Phase 1—internal pilot with manual chat entry and mandatory clinician acknowledgment of structured cards before acting on narrative; Phase 2—FHIR laboratory feed and Vietnamese synonym lexicon integrated; Phase 3—multi-site pilot with override logging and alert tuning; Phase 4—outcome study (GDMT optimization, readmission). Each phase preserves the architectural invariant that PostgreSQL catalogs and hard_block tiers remain authoritative.

Operational metrics should be tracked per phase: override rate on alerts (baseline 45% after optimization), time from chat send to `recommendation_ready`, percentage of turns triggering `missing_check`, catalog approval throughput for `needs_condition_refinement` rules, and clinician-reported trust in structured cards versus narrative. These process metrics align with how Osheroff's Five Rights evaluate CDSS success—right information, person, format, channel, time—without prematurely claiming outcome superiority.

Institutional adoption additionally requires training clinical_leads on admin portal approve/retire workflows, establishing governance committees for rule promotion, and defining medico-legal responsibility when recommendations inform but do not mandate prescribing. HF-CDSS explicitly keeps clinicians in the loop; deployment contracts should reflect that liability remains with treating physicians, while the system provides auditable decision support artifacts.

## 6.8 Closing Remarks

Heart failure remains one of the largest healthcare burdens worldwide and in Vietnam. Its treatment demands deep knowledge of multiple drug classes, interactions, and close monitoring of renal function, electrolytes, blood pressure, and heart rate. The CDSS proposed in this thesis is a concrete step toward helping physicians make accurate and timely treatment decisions without replacing clinical judgment.

With approximately 94% recommendation accuracy, 8.1 seconds average response time, 92.5% safety sensitivity, and 4.22/5 user satisfaction, the system shows clear potential to improve care quality when deployed as a clinician-supervised assistant grounded in governed catalogs and GraphRAG-retrieved evidence. Broader deployment will still require knowledge-base expansion with Vietnamese drug synonyms, FHIR integration for structured intake, and prospective clinical evaluation measuring outcomes rather than vignette accuracy alone.

The evaluation numbers tell a consistent story when read together rather than in isolation. Accuracy at 94.0% exceeds the 90% bar while leaving room for improvement on MRA recall (89.3%) where intake completeness matters most. Latency at 8.1 seconds mean and 7.4 seconds median meets ward-round tolerability for median cases even when P95 at 12.6 seconds signals tail cases needing streaming UX and future model optimization. Safety sensitivity at 92.5% paired with 100% pass on curated contraindication scenarios shows fail-closed design works for encoded hard rules—the more dangerous failure mode of silent contraindication miss is avoided. Satisfaction at 4.22/5 with clinical usefulness leading at 4.5/5 confirms the product direction: clinicians want GDMT gap and interaction support more than they demand perfect prose or sub-second total latency.

Knowledge construction metrics reinforce operational viability: 94.2% label extraction success, 95.0% section retention, and 96.6% LLM call avoidance at ingestion mean the knowledge base can evolve with labeling updates without prohibitive compute budgets. Rule classification—53.9% usable, 35.2% needing refinement—sets realistic expectations that automation produces drafts requiring clinical_lead governance, not oracle catalogs. Alert optimization from 8.2 to 4.3 per patient demonstrates that safety UX can be tuned without sacrificing hard blocks, addressing a historical reason CDSS adoption fails in practice.

Beyond a single product, the thesis offers a transferable methodological pattern—deterministic safety and GDMT logic, hybrid retrieval-augmented explanation, cost-aware LLM gating, bilingual deterministic simplification, and governance-oriented knowledge engineering—that can inform clinical decision support for other diseases and resource-constrained settings where accurate, explainable, and timely guidance is equally essential. The central lesson for practitioners and researchers is structural: in high-stakes pharmacotherapy domains, large language models excel as interfaces and explainers, not as silent authors of safety classification. Embedding that invariant in architecture—through PostgreSQL catalogs, hard_block tiers, verification agents, and SSE ordering that shows clinicians the verdict before the prose—transforms LLM capability from a liability into a supervised strength.

Heart failure, with its four GDMT pillars, dense interaction surface, and monitoring-dependent cautions, is an ideal proving ground for that pattern. The real-world GDMT implementation gap—only 1–2% of eligible patients on four drug classes at target doses—signals that decision support need not wait for perfect autonomous AI; even supervised, guideline-aligned nudges with accurate interaction checking may shift practice if embedded in workflow at 7–8 second median latency with Vietnamese–English accessibility. The evaluation reported here suggests the hybrid pattern works under controlled conditions. The limitations, threats to validity, and phased future work chart an honest path from research prototype to sustained clinical utility—one where clinicians remain authors of final decisions, but no longer bear alone the cognitive load of remembering every contraindication, titration step, and laboratory threshold that heart failure pharmacotherapy demands.

## 6.9 Epilogue: From Prototype to Practice

Translating HF-CDSS from thesis artifact to clinical practice is a social and organizational problem as much as a technical one. Hospitals adopt tools that fit existing workflows, respect liability norms, and reduce rather than increase documentation burden. The 4.5/5 clinical usefulness score suggests the GDMT gap and interaction checking value proposition resonates with cardiologists when presented through streaming chat and structured cards—a workflow closer to messaging a knowledgeable colleague than navigating static guideline PDFs. Realizing that value requires IT deployment, clinical governance committees, and honest communication that 94.0% structured accuracy is not 100% and that LLM paragraphs require the same skepticism clinicians apply to textbook prose.

Pilot deployments should start in educational or quality-improvement contexts—where GDMT gap review is the primary task—before expansion to high-acuity inpatient decision making. Training should emphasize reading `recommendation_ready` JSON fields on the clinical panel before accepting `answer_delta` narrative. Audit logs should be reviewed weekly for override patterns indicating rule misfit or alert fatigue recurrence above the optimized 4.3 alerts per patient baseline.

The hybrid pattern exported by this thesis—governed catalogs, cost-aware LLM gating, GraphRAG explanation, verification gates, bilingual deterministic UI—is intentionally boring in the safety path and intentionally rich in the explanation path. That asymmetry is the point. Heart failure pharmacotherapy is too unforgiving for end-to-end neural prescription; it is also too verbose for pure rule engines without natural language and evidence retrieval. The evaluation numbers in Section 6.8.1 demonstrate that the combination can be built, measured, and improved incrementally. The chapters that precede this conclusion supply the blueprints; the future work supplies the roadmap; the clinical community supplies the final verdict on whether supervised hybrid CDSS belongs at the bedside. This thesis argues it should be given that chance—carefully, governably, and with clinicians always in the loop.

Maintainers inheriting this codebase should treat PostgreSQL approved-rule catalogs and SSE event ordering as the contract surface most deserving of regression protection—changes there affect safety and clinician trust more than cosmetic UI adjustments. When in doubt, preserve fail-closed behavior and measure against the 50-case vignette suite and four safety scenarios before claiming improvement. That discipline keeps the hybrid method honest as models, guidelines, and formularies evolve.
