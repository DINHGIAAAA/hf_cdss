## 1.1 Background and Motivation

Heart failure (HF) is a long-term condition in which the heart muscle is too weak or too stiff to pump or fill well enough for normal daily activity, so the body does not receive the blood flow and oxygen it needs; it is a syndrome with many causes rather than a single disease, and global estimates put more than 64 million people living with HF worldwide, a figure that keeps rising as populations age and as more patients survive heart attacks and other acute events only to live with permanent damage to the pumping function of the heart [1]. People with HF commonly report shortness of breath on exertion or at rest, unusual tiredness, swelling in the legs or abdomen from retained fluid, and repeated admissions to hospital, and for health systems HF remains one of the leading reasons adults over 65 are hospitalized, with readmission within thirty days both frequent and costly. Clinicians usually classify HF by how effectively the left ventricle, the main pumping chamber, ejects blood with each heartbeat; **left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)** expresses that as a percentage, and when LVEF is 40% or lower the pattern is called **heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)**, which is the focus of this thesis because the drug regimens with the strongest outcome evidence in trials, collectively known as **guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT)**, have been studied most thoroughly in this group and, when started and titrated appropriately, reduce deaths and hospitalizations [2], [3]. Outpatient care for HFrEF is not a single prescription but ongoing adjustment across several drug classes: RAAS-blocking agents (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or the combined ARNI sacubitril/valsartan), beta blockers proven in HF trials, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), SGLT2 inhibitors, and often diuretics for congestion, and each class carries its own rules for eligibility, starting dose, uptitration pace, laboratory monitoring (kidney function, potassium, blood pressure), contraindications, and interactions with other classes, so omitting a pillar, titrating in an unsafe order, or missing a hard interaction can produce preventable harm, while even small increases in appropriate GDMT use can improve population outcomes when applied at scale [2], [3], [6]. **Clinical decision support systems (CDSS)** are software tools intended to present the right evidence at the moment of care; they are widely discussed as one way to shrink the well-documented gap between what guidelines recommend on paper and what busy clinicians actually prescribe and titrate in practice [4], [5]. In Vietnam the epidemiological picture resembles global trends but adds constraints that matter for system design: cardiology subspecialists concentrate in major urban centers, while provincial and district facilities rely more on general internists and family physicians who must interpret complex GDMT and safety rules with less immediate specialist backup [1], and clinical documentation often mixes Vietnamese and English, while medicines on local formularies appear under brand names that do not line up one-to-one with the international generic identifiers used in FDA labels and trial literature, which means a CDSS meant for Vietnamese practice cannot treat bilingual free-text intake and reliable drug-name normalization as optional extras. Two technical developments make a governed, explainable HF CDSS more realistic today than a decade ago: biomedical **knowledge graphs**, standard terminologies, and open repositories such as FDA DailyMed make it feasible to ingest authoritative label and interaction content into databases without manually re-keying entire formularies [7]-[11], while **large language models (LLMs)** make conversational interfaces over messy clinical notes practical, even though models that sound fluent can still state incorrect doses, invent drug names, or overlook absolute “do not combine” rules unless their outputs are constrained by fixed logic and retrieved sources [12], [19], and hosting open-weight models on hospital hardware reduces dependence on proprietary cloud APIs when pilot sites require patient vignettes and chat content to remain on premises. A heart-failure CDSS intended for supervised clinical use therefore needs approved safety rules and drug catalogs, search over guidelines and labels that can supply quotable evidence, and an LLM used mainly to parse ambiguous intake and explain structured results in plain language, because a chatbot alone cannot enforce hard stops on unsafe therapy and a traditional rule list alone cannot comfortably read narrative chat or attach guideline passages to each suggestion; Section 1.2 states purpose, scope, and approach, Section 1.3 narrows the operational problem and lists research questions, and Chapters 2 and 3 develop the theoretical and architectural detail behind that split between rules as authority and language models as explanation.

## 1.2 Research Purpose, Scope, and Approach

### 1.2.1 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this study is to design, implement, and evaluate a CDSS specialized for heart failure that wires together a medical **knowledge graph** (canonical drugs, conditions, and relationships), **GraphRAG** retrieval (search that combines text embeddings with graph neighborhood structure), fixed clinical rule engines whose outputs are repeatable and auditable, lightweight **verification** programs that re-check recommendations and citations against governed data, and a locally hosted LLM, so that clinicians can apply GDMT more consistently and safely by converting authoritative inputs, including FDA DailyMed structured product labels (SPL/XML), European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and American Heart Association / American College of Cardiology / Heart Failure Society of America (AHA/ACC/HFSA) heart-failure guidelines, and curated interaction knowledge, into structured rule catalogs and searchable text artifacts that both the reasoning layer and the explanation layer can use without contradicting each other. Scientifically the work follows four linked lines of inquiry: first, whether an automated **knowledge-construction pipeline** can extract constraint rules, dose rules, interaction rules, GDMT policies, and dose-safety warnings from heterogeneous documents with enough precision to populate PostgreSQL governance tables, ChromaDB vector indexes, and a Neo4j graph store; second, whether **hybrid retrieval** that runs dense embedding search, BM25 keyword search, graph traversal seeded from entities linked to the patient profile, HyDE-style query expansion, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to merge ranked lists can return citation-ready passages for HF queries where either keywords or embeddings alone often miss critical label wording [12]-[14]; third, whether separating fixed recommendation logic from LLM-generated narrative reduces hallucination risk while preserving a conversational doctor-facing interface; and fourth, how to define evaluation dimensions for accuracy, latency, safety behavior, and usability that Chapter 5 applies against predefined success criteria (Section 5.0). On the practical side the target is a cardiologist-supervised prototype that ingests SPL labels and guideline documents, accepts hybrid chat intake, runs GDMT gap analysis against PostgreSQL rules, performs interaction and dose-safety checks, uses GraphRAG to assemble evidence for explanations, runs verification agents on structured and narrative outputs, and streams bilingual recommendation and safety cards to a React dashboard through **Server-Sent Events (SSE)** so that critical structured content appears before longer explanatory text finishes generating, with explicit out-of-scope boundaries described in Section 1.2.2.

### 1.2.2 Scope and Delimitations

The primary clinical scope is pharmacologic GDMT for HFrEF, covering ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, evidence-based beta blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors, including initiation and uptitration, contraindication checking, and major interaction and dose-safety warnings derived from labels and guidelines, while HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), acute decompensated heart failure that requires intravenous vasoactive drugs, device therapy such as implantable defibrillators or resynchronization, transplantation, and dedicated palliative pathways are largely excluded so that the rule catalogs and evaluation vignettes can stay focused on the GDMT pillars with the clearest outpatient evidence. Knowledge sources are centered on FDA DailyMed SPL/XML, ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline corpora, and interaction and constraint tables maintained in PostgreSQL, and integration of a complete Vietnamese national formulary with local pricing and availability is treated as future work rather than a deliverable of this thesis. Patient information enters through doctor-typed or pasted chat rather than live HL7 or FHIR feeds from hospital information systems; all recommendations are advisory in the sense that hard “avoid” outcomes appear as structured alerts and statuses but prescribing authority remains with the clinician, deployment assumes locally hosted services with JWT authentication and Docker-based orchestration suitable for pilot environments, the dashboard supports Vietnamese and English with conversation context preserved when the user switches language, and evaluation relies on curated clinical vignettes and structured safety test suites rather than prospective randomized trials, which matches the goal of measuring guideline concordance, latency, fail-closed safety behavior, and usability under controlled conditions before any wider deployment study.

### 1.2.3 Thesis Statement

The central claim of the thesis is that a **hybrid CDSS**, coupling fixed GDMT and safety rules with knowledge-graph-augmented retrieval (GraphRAG) and locally hosted LLM explanation, can deliver accurate, timely, and bilingual heart-failure treatment support that fits clinical workflow while keeping clinicians in control of final decisions, where **accuracy** is understood as agreement between structured recommendation objects and expert-reviewed guideline expectations on test cases, **timeliness** as typical end-to-end response within about ten seconds on the evaluation hardware described in Chapter 5, and **safety** as fail-closed handling of hard contraindications recorded in governed catalogs together with verification agents that check structured outputs and cited evidence, and the design explicitly rejects both unconstrained generative chat that can invent therapy and rigid rule-only alerting that cannot read free-text intake or ground explanations in retrievable label and guideline text.

### 1.2.4 Knowledge Engineering Approach (Offline)

Knowledge engineering in this project spans an offline path from raw documents to synchronized stores that the online CDSS reads but does not mutate during a patient session: FDA DailyMed SPL XML drug labels and heart-failure guideline PDFs or web captures are acquired into versioned object storage, then a **three-tier section filter** discards clearly irrelevant material and limits expensive LLM calls by accepting sections with high-precision keyword matches on headings, scoring remaining sections with BGE-M3 semantic similarity against clinical prototypes, and sending only borderline sections to an LLM for a keep-or-drop decision. Text that passes filtering is split into sentence-aware chunks, passed through claim extraction so that each atomic statement can be traced to a source span, and classified into safety tiers labeled `hard_block` (must not be overridden by narrative), `usable_rules` (can drive or support recommendations after approval), and `needs_condition_refinement` (requires human review before production use), after which approved artifacts sync to PostgreSQL as the authoritative home for executable rules, to ChromaDB for dense vector retrieval, and to Neo4j for entity-relationship traversal during GraphRAG, with the explicit design rule that PostgreSQL hard blocks and approved dose and interaction rows always win over whatever passages vector or graph search might surface for explanation.

### 1.2.5 Query-Time Approach (Online)

At query time the system treats each doctor message as input to **hybrid intake** that merges regular-expression extraction of numeric labs and vitals (ejection fraction, eGFR, potassium, blood pressure, and similar fields), lexicon-based medication and condition recognition with negation handling so that phrases such as “not on ACE inhibitor” are not misread as active therapy, and selective LLM parsing when deterministic confidence is low, producing a structured patient profile that feeds a **reasoning engine** evaluating GDMT class coverage, applying constraint and interaction rules from PostgreSQL, and computing dose plans synchronously without using LLM generation to decide core start, continue, caution, or avoid statuses. In parallel, **GraphRAG** assembles explanatory context by optionally expanding the clinical question with HyDE into a hypothetical evidence paragraph before embedding, running retrievers concurrently over ChromaDB dense search, BM25 sparse search, and Neo4j neighborhood expansion from entities linked to the profile, fusing ranked hit lists with RRF, and passing the resulting chunks with citation metadata to verification agents and to the explanation LLM; the language model is not treated as the clinical authority but as a component that helps parse ambiguous intake, improves recall for retrieval, and writes readable narrative strictly conditioned on structured engine outputs and retrieved sources [18], [19], while verification agents check that hard avoid constraints from the rule engine appear in the streamed response and that quoted label or guideline text corresponds to chunks that were actually retrieved, and the React dashboard consumes SSE events so structured safety and GDMT cards reach the browser before the longer natural-language summary completes streaming.

### 1.2.6 Design Principle: LLM as Explanation Layer, Rules as Authority

The governing design principle is that approved deterministic rules and catalogs in PostgreSQL are the authority for recommendation statuses, hard blocks, and dose calculations, that retrieved evidence chunks with stable identifiers are the authority for what may be cited in explanation, and that LLMs serve as interface and language layer only, meaning they cannot downgrade a `hard_block` tier, invent medications or dose changes absent from rule-engine outputs, or present unsourced text as label fact; clinicians therefore see structured cards with explicit statuses first and streaming narrative second, and post-hoc review can trace each recommendation to PostgreSQL rule identifiers and each cited claim to retrieved chunk metadata, a division Chapter 3, Section 3.2.1 restates in the context of the full runtime architecture and module boundaries.

## 1.3 Problem Statement and Research Questions

Heart-failure therapy is among the most evidence-dense areas in outpatient medicine yet remains operationally fragile at the bedside, because the correct action for a given visit depends on HF phenotype (here HFrEF with reduced EF), the patient’s current drug list and allergies, recent laboratory values and vital signs, and sequencing rules such as mandatory washout between ACE inhibitors and ARNIs that are scattered across long guideline documents and product-specific FDA labels, so a useful CDSS must answer, for this patient at this moment, what GDMT gaps exist, what changes are safe to consider, and what combinations must be blocked, with no silent failure on the most serious contraindications. Real-world GDMT use at target doses in HFrEF still falls short of trial-era uptake [6], with wider gaps among older patients, those with renal impairment, and clinicians outside academic referral centers (Section 1.1), and daily practice adds cognitive load from dose math, electrolyte monitoring schedules, interaction checking across brand names, and, in Vietnam, bilingual charting and local naming conventions that do not map trivially to international drug keys. Pure chat-style generative AI accepts messy clinical prose but can hallucinate doses or miss absolute contraindications [18], [19]; **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** improves grounding by tying answers to indexed documents [12] but does not by itself enforce executable safety rules in a fail-closed way; classical rule engines remain auditable and testable [15], [4], [5] but are weak at conversational intake and at attaching readable, citable evidence to each alert. The technical problem addressed in this thesis is therefore integration: governed catalogs and fixed logic for what to recommend, hybrid search and graph structure for evidence, and LLMs limited to explanation and intake fallback, which motivates the following research questions.

**RQ1 (Knowledge pipeline):** How can an automated pipeline ingest FDA SPL labels, ESC/AHA/ACC/HFSA heart-failure guidelines, and interaction sources into governed constraint, dose, interaction, GDMT, and dose-safety catalogs, indexed for vector and graph retrieval, while controlling extraction cost and preserving clinical specificity through tiered section filtering and human-reviewable intermediate artifacts?

**RQ2 (Hybrid reasoning):** How can fixed GDMT and safety rule engines be combined with hybrid GraphRAG retrieval (HyDE expansion, BM25 sparse search, BGE-M3 dense search, Neo4j neighborhood traversal, RRF fusion, optional reranking) and verification agents so that structured recommendations remain guideline-concordant and fail closed on hard contraindications while LLM-generated explanations stay grounded in retrieved evidence?

**RQ3 (Bilingual UX and safety):** How can a chat-based Vietnamese–English clinical interface stream structured recommendation cards and plain-language summaries over SSE without losing conversation context when the user switches language, and how do accuracy, latency, alert sensitivity, and clinician satisfaction compare against predefined success criteria when evaluated on curated HFrEF vignettes?

## 1.4 Related Work (Summary)

Chapter 2 reviews prior work in full; at introductory level the thesis sits at the intersection of four research and practice lines, namely classical **CDSS** research and deployment experience with auditable rule bases and workflow-aware alerting [15], [4], [5], [17], biomedical **knowledge graphs** and controlled terminologies that represent drugs, diseases, and interactions as explicit linked entities rather than prose alone [7]-[11], modern **RAG and GraphRAG** methods that fuse dense embedding search, sparse lexical retrieval, and graph-constrained context before language-model generation [12]-[14], and heart-failure **clinical guidelines together with FDA SPL labels** that jointly supply normative GDMT recommendations and product-specific dosing, warning, and interaction language [2], [3], [6]. The gap this project targets is not any one of those ingredients in isolation but an integrated, HFrEF-focused, bilingual chat CDSS that pairs governed offline ingestion with hybrid GraphRAG, fixed GDMT and safety engines, verification agents on outputs and citations, and evaluation on structured recommendation objects rather than on free-text plausibility alone, with numeric success targets and measurement protocols defined in Chapter 5, Section 5.0.
