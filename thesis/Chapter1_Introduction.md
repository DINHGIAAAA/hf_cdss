# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

Heart failure (HF) is among the most consequential chronic cardiovascular conditions in modern medicine. Global epidemiological estimates indicate that more than 64 million people live with HF worldwide, and the prevalence continues to rise as populations age and as improved acute care allows more patients to survive myocardial infarction and other index events only to develop chronic ventricular dysfunction [1]. HF is not a single disease entity but a clinical syndrome characterized by structural or functional cardiac abnormalities that impair the heart's ability to fill or eject blood at pressures and volumes appropriate to metabolic demand. Patients experience dyspnea, fatigue, fluid retention, reduced exercise tolerance, and recurrent hospitalization; mortality remains high despite advances in device therapy and transplantation referral pathways. From a health-system perspective, HF is one of the leading causes of hospital admission among adults over 65, and readmission within 30 days is both common and costly, making HF a priority target for quality-improvement and decision-support interventions.

The syndrome is commonly classified by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) into HFrEF (LVEF ≤ 40%), HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), and HFpEF (LVEF ≥ 50%), with therapeutic emphasis in this thesis on HFrEF where GDMT pharmacotherapy has the strongest mortality evidence base [2], [3]. Natriuretic peptide elevation, structural heart disease on imaging, and compatible symptoms remain central to diagnosis, but once phenotype is established, management shifts toward long-term neurohormonal blockade, diuretic symptom control, comorbidity treatment, and serial reassessment of volume status and organ function. The chronicity of this trajectory explains why decision support must function across repeated encounters rather than as a one-shot admission tool.

The clinical and economic burden of HF extends well beyond inpatient episodes. Outpatient management requires longitudinal optimization of neurohormonal antagonists, diuretics, and increasingly SGLT2 inhibitors, with repeated laboratory monitoring, dose titration, and coordination across primary care and cardiology. Each medication class carries distinct initiation criteria, contraindications, interaction profiles, and monitoring obligations. When therapy is incomplete or incorrectly sequenced, patients face preventable morbidity: worsening congestion, arrhythmia, renal injury, hyperkalemia, and progression to advanced HF requiring inotropic support or mechanical circulatory support. Health economists therefore classify HF as a high-impact, high-cost chronic disease whose outcomes depend critically on whether clinicians can consistently translate trial evidence and practice guidelines into individualized, safe prescribing at the point of care.

Direct medical costs for HF include hospitalizations, emergency visits, imaging, laboratory monitoring, and long-term pharmacotherapy; indirect costs include lost productivity and caregiver burden. Quality-adjusted life years decline steeply as HF progresses from New York Heart Association class II symptoms to advanced stages requiring frequent diuretic adjustment and specialist escalation. Policymakers and hospital administrators therefore monitor HF readmission rates as a quality indicator, and clinical societies invest heavily in guideline updates because small improvements in GDMT penetration can shift population-level outcomes when multiplied across millions of patients. Information technology enters this picture not as a marginal convenience but as a scalable mechanism to compress the distance between published evidence and bedside action.

Epidemiological studies consistently rank HF among conditions with the highest lifetime healthcare expenditure per patient. Acute decompensation episodes trigger costly inpatient stays; suboptimal outpatient GDMT increases the frequency of these episodes. Conversely, each additional GDMT class initiated at appropriate doses is associated with incremental reductions in cardiovascular death and hospitalization in trial and observational data synthesized in ESC and AHA/ACC guidelines [2], [3]. The clinical motivation for CDSS is therefore anchored in population health economics as well as individual patient benefit: tools that help clinicians close GDMT gaps at scale address a modifiable driver of HF morbidity. Digital health strategies, electronic prescribing, laboratory integration, clinical decision support, and telemonitoring, are frequently cited in health-system modernization plans as complements to workforce expansion [4], [5].

In Vietnam, the HF burden mirrors global trends while presenting additional contextual constraints. Rapid epidemiological transition, aging demographics, rising hypertension and ischemic heart disease, and increasing diabetes prevalence, has expanded the pool of patients at risk for HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [1]. Cardiology capacity is unevenly distributed: tertiary centers in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City concentrate specialist expertise, whereas provincial hospitals and district health facilities rely more heavily on general internists and family physicians who manage complex GDMT decisions with fewer subspecialty resources. Vietnamese-language clinical documentation, local brand names for internationally recognized molecules, and heterogeneous familiarity with English-language guideline documents further complicate access to up-to-date, structured decision support.

Hospital-level heart-failure statistics from Vietnamese tertiary centers illustrate rising admission volumes and repeated readmissions among elderly patients with ischemic and hypertensive etiologies [1]. These patterns mirror the global observation that HF is as much a chronic outpatient management problem as an inpatient diagnostic label: the highest-yield interventions for mortality reduction occur during medication reconciliation and follow-up visits when GDMT classes are added or uptitrated. Decision support that reaches provincial clinicians in Vietnamese, at conversational speed, addresses an equity dimension of HF care, not only academic reference access for subspecialists.

National health reports document rising cardiovascular morbidity alongside infrastructure investment in hospital information systems, telemedicine, and artificial-intelligence research [1]. Yet adoption of specialized CDSS for chronic cardiovascular disease remains limited relative to administrative or billing systems. Clinicians in Vietnam frequently encounter the same GDMT knowledge clinicians face globally, quadruple therapy recommendations, ARNI washout rules, MRA potassium thresholds, but lack integrated tools that combine interaction checking, renal dose adjustment, and readable Vietnamese explanation in one workflow. Information technology therefore matters not as a substitute for clinical training but as a force multiplier that can bring guideline-concordant logic, interaction checking, and plain-language explanation to settings where specialist density and reference time are limited.

Research on artificial intelligence in Vietnamese healthcare notes growing interest in diagnostic imaging and administrative analytics, with comparatively fewer deployed systems for chronic pharmacotherapy guidance [1]. Language remains a first-class requirement: clinicians and patients often discuss medications using Vietnamese brand names, abbreviated vitals notation, and mixed Vietnamese-English charting conventions. A heart-failure CDSS intended for this context must therefore treat bilingual intake, deterministic card simplification, and governed drug normalization as core requirements rather than localization afterthoughts. The present study explicitly incorporates Vietnamese plain-language outputs and lexicon extensions alongside English, evaluating whether language switching preserves clinical context during multi-turn case discussions.

Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS) occupy a well-established role in addressing precisely this class of problem. Decades of informatics research demonstrate that timely, actionable computerized advice can improve practitioner performance on process measures relevant to chronic disease management, including medication selection, dosing, and monitoring [4], [5]. For HF specifically, the therapeutic standard has converged on Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT), a structured pharmacologic regimen organized around disease-modifying drug classes whose benefit is supported by large randomized trials and incorporated into international guidelines [2], [3], [6]. Yet real-world GDMT implementation remains poor: only about 1-2% of eligible HFrEF patients receive all four foundational drug classes at target doses in observational cohorts [6]. The gap between evidence and practice is not primarily one of ignorance at the guideline level; it is a workflow, cognition, and safety-complexity problem that information systems can help mitigate if they are accurate, fast, explainable, and integrated into how clinicians already communicate about cases.

The timing of this research is shaped by two converging technology trends. First, biomedical knowledge graphs, standardized terminologies, and open drug-label repositories have matured to the point where automated ingestion into governed catalogs is feasible without transcribing entire formularies by hand [7]-[11]. Second, large language models have made natural-language interfaces and retrieval-augmented explanation practical, but their well-documented hallucination risk makes them unsafe as standalone prescribers in pharmacologic domains [12]. Open-weight models served locally via Ollama reduce dependence on proprietary cloud APIs during hospital pilots where patient vignettes must remain on premises.

The present thesis is motivated by the conviction that heart-failure CDSS design must evolve beyond both brittle rule-only engines and unconstrained LLM chat interfaces. Rule-based systems offer auditability and deterministic safety but struggle with free-text intake, bilingual explanation, and flexible retrieval over heterogeneous guideline and labeling corpora. LLM chatbots offer natural interaction and summarization but, when used alone, can hallucinate doses, invent interactions, or omit hard contraindications that carry immediate patient harm. Hybrid composition is therefore not an optional enhancement but the central design response to complementary failure modes.

A hybrid architecture, coupling governed medical knowledge, deterministic GDMT and safety engines, Knowledge Graph-augmented retrieval (GraphRAG), and locally hosted LLM explanation, represents a principled response to this dual failure mode. The work described herein develops such a system for HFrEF-focused GDMT support, with Vietnamese and English interfaces, automated knowledge construction from FDA Structured Product Labels (SPL) and major HF guidelines, and evaluation against cardiologist-reviewed clinical vignettes. The overarching motivation is practical and scientific: to demonstrate that modern retrieval, graph, and language-model techniques can be composed into a clinician-supervised assistant that improves decision speed and safety without displacing professional judgment.

The pharmacologic scope aligns with contemporary quadruple-therapy recommendations. Renin-angiotensin system inhibition may be delivered through ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or ARNIs depending on tolerability, prior exposure, and blood pressure; beta blockers require agent-specific evidence in HFrEF and careful uptitration; MRAs add neurohormonal blockade with mandatory renal and potassium safeguards; SGLT2 inhibitors extend benefits across diabetic and non-diabetic HFrEF cohorts [2], [3], [6]. Each class interacts with the others and with common comorbidity medications such as diuretics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and potassium supplements. A CDSS that treats these classes as independent lookup tables misses the clinical reality that GDMT decisions are jointly constrained, a theme that recurs in the problem statement (Section 1.3), technical approach (Section 1.6), and evaluation design (Section 1.7).

## 1.2 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this study is to design, implement, and evaluate a Clinical Decision Support System specialized for heart failure that integrates a medical Knowledge Graph, hybrid GraphRAG retrieval, deterministic clinical rule engines, verification agents, and a locally hosted large language model. The system is intended to help clinicians apply Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy more consistently and safely by transforming authoritative sources, FDA DailyMed SPL/XML, ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA clinical guidelines, and curated interaction knowledge, into governed, queryable artifacts that support both structured reasoning and evidence-grounded explanation.

Scientifically, the study pursues several interconnected objectives. First, it investigates how an automated knowledge-construction pipeline can extract constraint rules, dose rules, interaction rules, GDMT policies, and dose-safety warnings from heterogeneous medical documents with sufficient precision to populate PostgreSQL governance catalogs, ChromaDB vector indexes, and Neo4j graph stores without manual transcription of entire formularies. Second, it examines how hybrid retrieval, combining dense embedding search, BM25 sparse search, graph neighborhood traversal, HyDE query expansion, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), can supply citation-ready context for heart-failure queries where neither lexical nor semantic retrieval alone is reliable [12]-[14]. Third, it evaluates whether a deliberate architectural separation between deterministic recommendation logic and LLM-mediated narrative reduces hallucination risk while preserving conversational usability. Fourth, it measures end-to-end system behavior on recommendation accuracy, latency, safety-alert sensitivity, bilingual usability, and clinician satisfaction using predefined success criteria developed before implementation.

A fifth objective concerns maintainability: demonstrate that clinical leads can approve, refine, or retire extracted rules through admin interfaces and trigger index resynchronization without developer intervention for each label or guideline update. Without maintainability, even a strong prototype decays as sources change, a common failure mode of academic CDSS demonstrations that this thesis explicitly seeks to avoid through governed catalogs and idempotent pipeline reruns.

From a scientific-method perspective, the study treats the CDSS as an artifact whose claims are testable: if deterministic rules and hybrid retrieval are correctly composed, then structured outputs should align with cardiologist-reviewed guideline expectations at measurable rates, hard contraindications should not be silently dropped, and explanation should cite retrievable evidence. The knowledge pipeline is evaluated not only by coverage statistics but by the proportion of extracted artifacts that reach executable tiers versus those flagged for human refinement, reflecting realistic expectations that automated extraction produces drafts requiring governance rather than flawless autonomous codification.

Reproducibility is an explicit scientific objective. Versioned raw and processed artifacts in object storage, deterministic pipeline stages with content-hash idempotency, and environment variables prefixed with `HF_CDSS_` allow independent replication of ingestion and evaluation runs on comparable hardware. Reporting accuracy, latency, and satisfaction metrics against predefined thresholds before inspecting results reduces the risk of post-hoc criterion shifting, a concern in design-science evaluations where prototypes evolve iteratively during development.

Practically, the study aims to deliver a working prototype suitable for cardiologist-supervised use in clinical workflow rather than a purely theoretical architecture. Concretely, the implementation targets automated ingestion and classification of SPL and guideline content through a cost-aware three-tier section filter (keyword matching, BGE-M3 semantic similarity, borderline LLM review); conversion of free-text clinical chat into a structured `PatientProfile` via hybrid intake combining regular-expression extraction, lexicon-based entity detection, negation handling, unit normalization, and selective LLM parsing; personalized GDMT gap analysis and medication recommendations governed by PostgreSQL-backed rules; enforcement of interaction, contraindication, renal, electrolyte, and dose-safety checks before advice is displayed; parallel GraphRAG assembly of explanatory evidence; multi-agent verification of recommendation consistency; and streaming delivery of bilingual plain-language summaries through a React doctor dashboard using Server-Sent Events (SSE).

The practical purpose aligns with outpatient and clinic settings where a physician discusses a case, types or dictates clinical details into chat, and needs a rapid structured view of GDMT gaps, safety conflicts, and suggested next steps before placing orders. The system is designed for local deployment via Docker Compose with Ollama-hosted models so that institutions concerned about cloud privacy or intermittent connectivity can still run inference on premises. Admin workflows allow clinical leads to approve, edit, or retire extracted rules without redeploying application code, a practical requirement because labeling and guideline revisions are continuous.

Stakeholder-oriented purposes can be distinguished without conflating them. For treating clinicians, the system aims to reduce search time across labels and guidelines, surface forgotten contraindications, and standardize GDMT gap review. For clinical pharmacists and quality officers, governed catalogs and audit logs support medication-use evaluation and rule curation. For informatics researchers, the pipeline and hybrid retrieval stack provide a reproducible artifact demonstrating how LLMs can be constrained in high-risk domains. None of these purposes substitute for regulatory approval, prospective outcome trials, or institution-specific protocol governance where local formularies diverge from ingested sources.

The study does not aim to replace electronic health record systems, automate prescribing without human approval, or provide diagnostic certainty for acute decompensated heart failure requiring emergent intervention. Its purpose is narrower and more defensible: to show that a hybrid CDSS can operationalize a substantial fraction of outpatient HFrEF GDMT knowledge in a form clinicians can interact with through chat, trust through deterministic safety tiers, and evaluate through transparent metrics aligned with guideline concordance and alert burden.

Methodologically, the study follows a design-science paradigm common in medical informatics: articulate a problem grounded in clinical evidence and workflow observation; propose an artifact architecture with testable claims; implement the artifact with reproducible engineering practices; and evaluate against predefined criteria using expert review, timed benchmarks, and structured safety suites. This paradigm aligns scientific rigor with the practical necessity of delivering deployable software rather than purely conceptual models.

## 1.3 Problem of the Study

The problem addressed by this thesis sits at the intersection of a persistent clinical care gap, limitations of existing technical approaches, and open research questions about how to combine them responsibly. Heart-failure therapy is simultaneously evidence-rich and operationally fragile: the correct action depends on phenotype, current medications, laboratory values, vital signs, and sequencing constraints that span multiple documents and update cycles. Any decision-support solution must therefore solve not only an algorithmic retrieval problem but a governance, safety, and human-factors problem in bilingual, resource-variable clinical environments.

### 1.3.1 Clinical Problem (GDMT Underuse, Barriers)

For HFrEF, contemporary practice guidelines organize disease-modifying therapy around four GDMT pillars: renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors), evidence-based beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors [2], [3], [6]. Each class has trial-supported mortality and morbidity benefit; guideline committees now emphasize early initiation and rapid uptitration rather than sequential delay that was common in earlier eras. Despite this strong evidence base, real-world implementation remains strikingly incomplete. Observational data indicate that only about 1-2% of eligible patients receive all four classes at target doses [6], and many more never initiate one or more indicated classes at any dose. Underuse is not randomly distributed: older patients, those with renal impairment, patients cared for outside academic centers, and those followed primarily by generalists show larger gaps. The clinical problem is therefore not merely underprescribing in aggregate but systematic failure to individualize GDMT safely and completely across diverse patient profiles.

Several interlocking barriers explain this gap. First, information overload exceeds what busy clinicians can continuously synthesize. Guideline documents span hundreds of pages; drug labels contain nuanced renal adjustments, potassium warnings, and interaction language; and trial publications continue to refine class sequencing and comorbidity exceptions. A generalist managing dozens of chronic conditions cannot maintain mental models of every washout interval, every contraindicated combination, and every lab threshold that modifies eligibility. Second, absolute contraindications and softer cautions depend on dynamic patient state. Initiating an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor within 36 hours of ACE inhibitor exposure, starting mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists when serum potassium or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) lies outside safe bounds, or uptitrating beta blockers in the setting of symptomatic hypotension or bradycardia each requires integrating medications, labs, and vitals that may be scattered across chat fragments, referral letters, or incomplete intake forms.

Third, dosing complexity adds error risk. Starting doses, target doses, and titration schedules must be individualized by eGFR, serum potassium, blood pressure, and heart rate; textual guidelines describe principles, but point-of-care tools often lack executable dose catalogs tied to the same contraindication logic. Fourth, workforce geography matters. Where cardiologist density is low, as in many Vietnamese provincial settings, clinicians lack rapid access to structured decision support at the moment titration or class addition is considered. Passive PDF guidelines do not alert; memory alone does not check interactions; and informal consultation is asynchronous.

Fifth, therapeutic inertia and care fragmentation across primary and specialist settings mean GDMT optimization stalls even when individual clinicians possess adequate knowledge. Discharge summaries may list "consider SGLT2i" without explicit orders; primary care follow-up may focus on blood pressure without class-level GDMT accounting; and specialist visits may occur too infrequently to drive uptitration. A CDSS cannot replace care coordination, but it can standardize gap review at each touchpoint where medications are discussed, reducing dependence on perfect handoffs between teams.

Consider a representative outpatient vignette: a 68-year-old man with HFrEF (LVEF 30%), hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and stage 3 chronic kidney disease presents for follow-up on lisinopril 10 mg and carvedilol 12.5 mg twice daily. He is not on an MRA or SGLT2 inhibitor; potassium is 4.9 mmol/L; eGFR is 38 mL/min/1.73 m²; blood pressure is 108/68 mmHg. The correct GDMT plan requires simultaneous assessment of whether to add dapagliflozin, whether spironolactone is appropriate given potassium and renal function, whether beta-blocker uptitration is hemodynamically safe, and whether switching from ACE inhibitor to ARNI should be discussed with explicit washout planning. Each decision depends on cross-cutting constraints. A CDSS must surface these dependencies coherently rather than returning four isolated monograph snippets.

Similar complexity arises in ARNI transitions: a patient stable on ramipril may benefit from sacubitril/valsartan, but only after a mandatory ACE inhibitor washout window measured from the last dose [2], [3]. Potassium 4.9 mmol/L may be acceptable for careful MRA initiation with close monitoring in some patients yet trigger caution or delay in others depending on concurrent diuretic regimen and historical hyperkalemia. SGLT2 inhibitors add cardiorenal benefit but require attention to volume status, genital infection counseling, and concomitant diuretic adjustment. These are not edge cases, they are routine outpatient decisions where incomplete synthesis causes therapeutic inertia or unsafe shortcuts.

These barriers produce predictable failure modes: delayed initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors despite HFrEF indication; continued ACE inhibitor use without planned washout before ARNI transition; MRA prescription without adequate potassium monitoring context; beta-blocker uptitration despite hemodynamic instability; and therapeutic inertia after hospital discharge when GDMT optimization is most valuable. A CDSS for heart failure must therefore address not only "what does the guideline recommend in general?" but "what should happen for this patient now, given these labs, these drugs, and these risks?", with explicit safety enforcement for conditions where error is unacceptable.

### 1.3.2 Technical Problem (LLM Hallucination vs Rigid Rules)

Purely generative LLM chatbots are attractive for natural-language interaction. They accept messy clinical descriptions, infer implicit context, and produce fluent explanations that reduce cognitive load for busy clinicians. Recent biomedical language-model research demonstrates substantial encoded clinical knowledge and strong performance on medical question-answering benchmarks when appropriately prompted. However, probabilistic generation is fundamentally misaligned with the safety requirements of medication decision support when used as the sole authority. LLMs can hallucinate plausible but incorrect doses, invent drug-drug interactions not present in any retrieved source, omit hard contraindications stated clearly in labeling text, or conflate HFpEF and HFrEF recommendations. They may also produce English-dominant outputs that lose nuance when translated or summarized for Vietnamese-speaking clinicians and patients. In regulated, high-stakes prescribing contexts, a single false negative on an absolute "avoid" rule is clinically catastrophic even if average answer quality appears high.

Empirical evaluations of general-purpose chat models on medication questions reveal non-trivial error rates on dosing and contraindication items that appear straightforward in labeling text. The errors are particularly insidious because they are fluent: a wrong starting dose stated confidently can bypass the skepticism clinicians apply to obviously broken rule-engine alerts. Retrieval-Augmented Generation mitigates but does not eliminate this risk if the generator ignores retrieved passages, if retrieval misses the single sentence stating an absolute prohibition, or if the system lacks a deterministic enforcement layer that blocks unsafe structured outputs regardless of narrative tone [12].

Comparison with rule engines highlights complementary gaps. Rules encode what must never happen; retrieval surfaces why guidelines recommend an action; LLMs explain both to clinicians in conversational prose. Removing any leg collapses the tripod: rules alone feel opaque; retrieval alone does not prescribe; LLMs alone are unsafe. The technical problem is therefore integrative rather than algorithmic in the narrow sense, success depends on architecture and governance at least as much as on choosing a stronger base model.

Purely rule-based CDSS systems occupy the opposite pole. Systems in the tradition of MYCIN demonstrated that explicit if-then logic with certainty factors could support infectious-disease therapy reasoning with auditable inference trails [15]. Later enterprise and EHR-embedded rule engines extended this paradigm to drug-drug interaction alerting, duplicate therapy detection, and dosing range checks. Systematic reviews confirm benefits of computerized decision support for practitioner performance and, in some chronic-disease settings, process outcomes [4], [5]. Rule engines excel where knowledge can be encoded precisely and where outputs must be reproducible. Their weaknesses, however, are equally well documented. Free-text intake is brittle without robust natural-language understanding; maintaining large rule bases across guideline revisions is labor-intensive; explaining why a rule fired in clinician-friendly language often requires a separate narrative layer; and retrieving supporting evidence passages from long documents is not a native capability of flat rule tables.

Heart-failure rules additionally require **conditional arithmetic** over labs and vitals, eGFR thresholds, potassium cutoffs, blood-pressure floors for beta-blocker uptitration, not merely Boolean drug-drug pairs. Encoding such logic in maintainable form motivates PostgreSQL JSONB dose and constraint catalogs with explicit predicates rather than hardcoding every combination in application source. The thesis pipeline automates candidate rule extraction from labels and guidelines, but human governance remains responsible for promoting only validated rows to executable tiers, a division of labor that scales better than manual rule authorship from scratch for thousands of label-derived statements.

Alert fatigue exemplifies the human-factors cost of naive rule deployment: when specificity is low, clinicians override alerts, and even high-sensitivity rules lose effectiveness. Heart-failure rules are especially prone to tension between sensitivity and burden because many label statements are conditional ("use with caution if...") rather than absolute. A technically correct system that fires eight soft warnings per patient may be rejected in practice; one that suppresses warnings to reduce noise may miss hard contraindications. The technical problem therefore includes calibrating tiered severity, hard blocks versus cautions, and pairing rules with evidence citations that help clinicians accept or reject alerts quickly.

Maintaining rule currency poses a second technical challenge. FDA labels and ESC/AHA guidelines update on independent schedules; a rule extracted from a 2021 label may conflict with a 2024 guideline addendum if not re-ingested and re-governed. Manual rule editing alone does not scale to thousands of constraints; fully autonomous LLM rewrites without human approval are unsafe. The thesis addresses maintenance through versioned pipeline artifacts, admin retire/approve workflows, and explicit `needs_condition_refinement` tiers that queue imperfect extractions for human review rather than silently deploying them as hard blocks.

The technical problem, then, is how to combine structured medical knowledge, deterministic safety logic, and LLM-mediated interaction so that heart-failure decision support remains accurate, explainable, and clinically usable. Neither paradigm alone satisfies the requirements: rules without retrieval and language struggle with evidence citation and conversational intake; LLMs without governed rules and verified retrieval struggle with safety and auditability. The thesis treats this as an integration problem requiring explicit role assignment, deterministic engines as authority for recommendations and hard constraints; retrieval and graphs as authority for evidence grounding; LLMs as authority only for explanation, borderline disambiguation, and intake fallback where measured extraction is insufficient.

### 1.3.3 Research Questions

Three research questions organize the investigation:

**RQ1 (Knowledge pipeline):** How can an automated pipeline ingest FDA SPL labels, ESC/AHA/ACC/HFSA heart-failure guidelines, and interaction sources into governed constraint, dose, interaction, GDMT, and dose-safety catalogs, indexed for both vector and graph retrieval, while controlling extraction cost and preserving clinical specificity through tiered filtering and human-reviewable artifacts?

**RQ2 (Hybrid reasoning):** How can deterministic GDMT and safety rule engines be combined with hybrid GraphRAG retrieval (HyDE expansion, BM25 sparse search, BGE-M3 dense search, Neo4j neighborhood traversal, RRF fusion, optional reranking) and verification agents so that structured recommendations remain guideline-concordant and fail-closed on hard contraindications while LLM-generated explanations stay grounded in retrieved evidence?

**RQ3 (Bilingual UX and safety):** How can a chat-based, Vietnamese-English clinical interface stream structured recommendation cards and plain-language summaries over SSE without losing conversation context, and how do accuracy, latency, alert sensitivity, and clinician satisfaction compare against predefined success criteria when evaluated on curated HFrEF vignettes?

RQ1 is primarily an offline knowledge-engineering question: it asks whether multi-source medical text can be transformed into governed, indexed artifacts at acceptable cost and quality. RQ2 is a runtime reasoning-and-grounding question: it asks whether structured clinical decisions can remain deterministic while explanations leverage hybrid retrieval and verification. RQ3 is a human-factors and systems question: it asks whether the composed artifact meets workflow, language, and performance thresholds defined before implementation. Answering all three is necessary to claim that the HF-CDSS is not merely a collection of strong components but an integrated system suitable for cardiologist-supervised use.

These questions are deliberately scoped to HFrEF GDMT and pharmacologic safety rather than full HF management including devices, transplantation, palliative pathways, or acute hemodynamic stabilization. They emphasize engineering reproducibility (pipeline idempotency, catalog governance, measurable latency) and clinical process outcomes (guideline concordance, alert burden, user satisfaction) rather than prospective mortality trials, which lie outside the thesis timeline but represent important future validation. RQ1 addresses sustainability of the knowledge base; RQ2 addresses correctness and grounding of recommendations; RQ3 addresses whether the combined system is usable and trustworthy in the intended bilingual clinical setting.

Subordinate hypotheses inform each research question without replacing them. For RQ1, the working hypothesis is that a three-tier section filter plus structured claim extraction can populate governed catalogs with greater than ninety percent section retention while invoking LLM review on less than ten percent of ingested sections. For RQ2, the working hypothesis is that RRF-fused hybrid retrieval plus deterministic rule engines yields higher guideline concordance and lower contraindication miss rates than LLM-only or vector-only baselines on the same vignette set. For RQ3, the working hypothesis is that SSE-delivered structured cards combined with bilingual deterministic simplification yield clinician satisfaction at or above four on a five-point Likert scale while maintaining median latency below ten seconds. Chapter 5 reports whether these hypotheses are supported, partially supported, or refuted by empirical results.

## 1.4 Related Work and Research Gap

Heart-failure CDSS development does not occur in a vacuum. It inherits decades of medical informatics research on rule-based expert systems, knowledge-graph construction, biomedical terminologies, retrieval-augmented language models, and international HF guidelines. The subsections below situate the present work within that literature and articulate the specific gap the thesis targets.

### 1.4.1 Classical CDSS

Classical CDSS research established foundational principles still relevant to modern systems. MYCIN demonstrated rule-based infectious-disease therapy advice with explicit uncertainty representation, showing that encoded clinical heuristics could match expert performance in narrow domains when knowledge engineering was rigorous [15]. Subsequent systems, DXplain, QMR, Internist-1, and EHR-embedded alerting platforms, extended decision support across broader diagnostic and therapeutic scopes. Osheroff's "Five Rights" framework emphasizes that clinical value depends on delivering the right information to the right person in the right format through the right channel at the right time, a reminder that accuracy alone is insufficient if alerts arrive too late, target the wrong role, or produce unsustainable alert burden.

For heart failure specifically, the Five Rights translate into concrete design requirements. The right information includes LVEF phenotype, current GDMT class coverage, potassium and eGFR, blood pressure, heart rate, and active medications including recently stopped ACE inhibitors relevant to washout. The right person is typically the prescribing physician or clinical pharmacist at medication reconciliation or follow-up. The right format combines structured status indicators (initiate, uptitrate, defer, avoid) with plain-language rationale and citation anchors. The right channel in this thesis is a dedicated doctor dashboard with SSE streaming rather than passive EHR inbox burial. The right time is during case discussion when titration or class addition is actively considered, not hours after orders are placed.

Systematic reviews of computerized clinical decision support report improvements in practitioner performance and, for some chronic-disease management interventions, favorable effects on process measures [4], [5]. Medication reconciliation and chronic-disease CDSS studies similarly suggest that well-designed prompts at prescribing and monitoring moments can narrow evidence-practice gaps. Nevertheless, classical deployments also illustrate recurring failure modes: alert fatigue from low positive predictive value, poor workflow integration, opaque rule maintenance, and user distrust when systems cannot explain why a recommendation appeared. Heart-failure GDMT amplifies these tensions because recommendations are longitudinal, conditional on labs, and intertwined with class sequencing constraints that span months of care.

Enterprise CDSS products embedded in commercial EHRs often excel at medication interaction checking against proprietary drug compendia but provide limited GDMT gap analysis tailored to HF phenotyping. Research prototypes from academic medical centers frequently focus on single tasks, readmission prediction, generic interaction alerting, or static guideline portals, without integrating ingestion, governed rule maintenance, and conversational intake in one maintainable stack. Chronic-disease CDSS reviews specifically note that sustained impact depends on integrating support into recurring outpatient visits rather than one-time inpatient alerts [5]. Heart-failure GDMT optimization is inherently longitudinal: initiation, uptitration, laboratory recheck, and class addition may span multiple encounters over weeks or months. A chat-based CDSS that accumulates conversation context and re-evaluates GDMT coverage as new labs appear in later messages approximates this longitudinal workflow more naturally than isolated pop-up alerts, provided intake reliably updates the structured profile across turns. This thesis therefore treats multi-turn chat not as a cosmetic interface choice but as a workflow hypothesis testable through bilingual context-preservation criteria in Section 1.7.

### 1.4.2 Knowledge Graphs and Biomedical Resources

Knowledge graphs represent entities and relations explicitly, enabling multi-hop queries that traverse clinical pathways rather than relying on lexical coincidence alone [7], [8]. In healthcare, standardized terminologies and curated resources reduce fragmentation across sources: the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) integrates biomedical vocabularies [9]; SNOMED CT provides clinical concept hierarchies [10]; and DrugBank, RxNorm, and related pharmacology databases supply drug properties, interactions, and identifiers usable for entity linking [11]. Graph-based drug-drug interaction resources further demonstrate that relational structure supports safety queries beyond flat synonym lists.

Knowledge graph embedding and relational machine-learning methods learn vector representations of entities and relations for link prediction and completion [7], [8]. While the thesis does not rely on black-box embedding-based prediction to fire clinical alerts, it uses explicit graph traversals and human-governed triples for interpretability. This design choice favors auditable paths (drug → interacts_with → potassium_sparing_agent → raises_risk → hyperkalemia) over opaque scores that clinicians cannot inspect during medicolegal review, a recurring theme in regulated clinical software.

Knowledge graph refinement surveys emphasize that automated extraction introduces noise, duplicate entities, uncertain relations, outdated edges, and that governance workflows are as important as extraction algorithms [8]. Paulheim's refinement taxonomy distinguishes correcting erroneous facts, completing missing links, and detecting outdated knowledge. For a CDSS ingesting FDA labels and guidelines that change on independent schedules, these refinement concerns are operational requirements, not optional polish. The thesis responds by storing rules in PostgreSQL with draft, approved, and retired lifecycle states and by preserving JSONL provenance linking each rule to source passages.

For heart failure, a knowledge graph can encode not only that beta blockers treat HFrEF but that specific agents carry evidence in reduced ejection fraction, that ACE inhibitors contraindicate immediate ARNI transition without washout, that MRAs require potassium and renal monitoring, and that SGLT2 inhibitors confer benefit even in patients without diabetes. Multi-hop paths connect patient state to contraindicated actions in ways that isolated document chunks may not surface if evidence is scattered across label sections and guideline chapters. The thesis builds on this tradition by importing pipeline-extracted entities and relations into Neo4j and by using graph neighborhoods as one retrieval channel in hybrid GraphRAG, complementing, not replacing, governed PostgreSQL rule catalogs that enforce executable constraints at query time.

Entity linking remains a practical bottleneck: surface strings in clinical chat may refer to brand names, salt forms, or abbreviated Vietnamese labels that must map to canonical drug nodes before interaction and GDMT logic execute reliably [9]-[11]. The intake lexicon and normalization layer therefore perform a lightweight linking step from detected strings to internal drug keys aligned where possible with RxNorm-style identifiers used in interaction rules. Imperfect linking is an known limitation documented in evaluation; expanding Vietnamese synonym catalogs is prioritized as future work rather than hidden as implementation detail.

### 1.4.3 RAG, GraphRAG, HyDE, RRF

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) conditions LLM outputs on externally retrieved passages, reducing reliance on parametric memory and improving grounding for knowledge-intensive tasks [12]. In clinical settings, grounding supports audit trails: each narrative claim can, in principle, be traced to retrieved evidence chunks with source metadata. Dense retrieval uses bi-encoder embeddings to match paraphrases and semantic equivalents; sparse retrieval using BM25 excels at exact drug names, lab units, and regulatory phrases. Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges ranked lists from heterogeneous retrievers without requiring score calibration, making it attractive when dense and sparse methods exhibit complementary failure modes, as they do for clinical queries that blend conceptual intent ("Can I start MRA in advanced CKD?") with lexical anchors (spironolactone, eGFR 22).

Chunking strategy materially affects RAG quality in medical corpora. Overly large chunks dilute relevance signals; overly small chunks lose conditional statements that span sentences (for example, renal thresholds stated in one sentence and dose adjustments in the next). The ingestion pipeline therefore uses sentence-aware windows near 512 tokens with overlap, preserving local coherence for warning and dosing sections. Metadata tags record drug class, document source (label versus guideline), and section type to support pre-filtering at query time, an engineering choice that reduces retrieval noise without hardcoding clinical logic into the vector index alone.

GraphRAG extends RAG by incorporating graph neighborhoods and, in community summarization variants, higher-level cluster context for multi-hop reasoning over narrative corpora [13]. For private or domain-specific medical collections, graph-augmented retrieval can surface related constraints and drug-class relations that pure vector similarity misses when wording diverges across documents. HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) further expands underspecified queries by generating a hypothetical answer document before embedding-based retrieval, improving recall when clinician questions are short relative to the evidence corpus [14]. Short queries such as "Start MRA?" lack the lexical overlap of label paragraphs that discuss spironolactone, eplerenone, hyperkalemia, and renal function; HyDE synthetically densifies the query embedding space before nearest-neighbor search.

Verification agents complement retrieval grounding by checking internal consistency: does the narrative mention a guideline section that was not retrieved? Does a recommended uptitration contradict a fired hard block? Does an interaction alert reference drugs not present in the normalized profile? These checks implement a second line of defense after deterministic rules, catching composition errors that individual modules might pass in isolation. Verification uses lightweight models where possible to preserve latency budgets established in Section 1.7.

The thesis adopts HyDE, parallel dense and sparse retrieval, Neo4j traversal, and RRF fusion as query-time techniques to assemble citation-ready context for explanation and verification, while explicitly withholding from the LLM the power to override hard deterministic blocks. Optional cross-encoder or API-based reranking can improve precision on the fused candidate set when latency budgets allow. Biomedical language models such as BioBERT and clinical foundation models demonstrate the value of domain-adapted representations for entity recognition, relation extraction, and question answering, while also underscoring residual risks of bias, dataset shift, and hallucination that demand grounding and human oversight. The present work uses embedding models (BGE-M3) and instruct LLMs (Qwen2.5) locally via Ollama for cost control, data sovereignty, and predictable deployment, but assigns them subordinate roles relative to governed catalogs and verification agents.

Surveys unifying LLMs and knowledge graphs articulate a roadmap in which graphs supply structure and LLMs supply language interface [13]. From an information-retrieval perspective, the hybrid stack addresses known failure modes of each component in isolation. Dense retrieval alone may return semantically similar but clinically irrelevant passages, for example, HFpEF discussion when the patient has HFrEF, unless metadata filters or graph seeds constrain the candidate pool. BM25 alone may miss paraphrased contraindications that do not share query tokens. Graph traversal alone cannot surface nuanced prose from guideline narrative sections unless those passages were also chunked and linked to graph entities during ingestion. RRF fusion provides a robust merge strategy when no single retriever dominates across query types, which is expected in conversational CDSS where user utterances vary from terse orders to detailed case presentations [12], [14].

### 1.4.4 HF Guidelines (ESC, AHA/ACC/HFSA)

International heart-failure guidelines define the clinical standard of care for diagnosis, phenotyping, and pharmacologic management. The 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure consolidate evidence on HFrEF quadruple therapy, comorbidity management, and monitoring requirements [2]. The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline similarly emphasizes GDMT optimization, SGLT2 inhibitor integration, and practical pathways for medication initiation and titration [3]. These documents provide the normative backbone against which CDSS recommendations are evaluated in this thesis: class indications, sequencing considerations, contraindications, and monitoring recommendations are extracted into machine-readable policies and cross-checked during verification.

Both guideline families converge on the four foundational pharmacologic classes for HFrEF while differing in organizational structure, terminology, and emphasis on comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, iron deficiency, and cardiorenal syndrome. A CDSS intended for bilingual use in settings where clinicians may reference either ESC or AHA/ACC traditions must encode class-level policies that align with both where evidence converges and document explicit provenance where recommendations diverge in nuance. GDMT optimization pathways published as ACC expert consensus decision pathways further refine practical uptitration sequences and are treated as supplementary policy inputs where they clarify sequencing without contradicting core class indications [3].

Key guideline themes encoded as GDMT policies in the system include: early initiation of quadruple therapy when tolerated; preference for ARNI over ACE inhibitor or ARB in eligible HFrEF patients willing to switch with appropriate washout; beta-blocker use only with agents proven in HFrEF trials; MRA initiation with potassium and renal monitoring; and SGLT2 inhibitor benefit independent of diabetes status [2], [3]. Acute decompensation, severe hyperkalemia, symptomatic hypotension, and angioedema history modify these themes through constraint rules rather than through narrative override, preserving deterministic evaluation even when guideline prose is nuanced.

Guidelines alone, however, are not executable software. They are prose artifacts requiring interpretation, localization to patient labs and medications, and continual revision as new trials publish. FDA SPL labels complement guidelines with legally authoritative dosing, warning, and interaction language for specific products. A heart-failure CDSS must reconcile both: guidelines for class-level GDMT strategy; labels for product-specific dose forms, renal adjustments, and contraindication wording. The thesis pipeline ingests both source families into shared governance artifacts rather than treating guidelines as the only ground truth or labels as the only constraint source.

### 1.4.5 Research Gap for This Thesis

Despite substantial progress across CDSS, knowledge graphs, RAG, GraphRAG, and HF guidelines, several gaps persist in deployed and research systems relevant to this work. Many commercial or research CDSS products remain oncology-focused, English-only, relatively slow, or limited to passive document lookup without governed dosing calculators and reviewable safety catalogs. LLM-centric medical chat prototypes often prioritize conversational fluency over fail-closed contraindication enforcement, structured GDMT gap analysis, and bilingual deterministic card summaries. Vector-only RAG implementations may retrieve plausible passages yet miss interaction chains best expressed as graph paths or executable rules. Conversely, rule-only systems may fire correct alerts yet fail to cite the evidentiary provenance clinicians expect when justifying therapy changes to patients and colleagues.

Comparative reviews of prominent systems illustrate the fragmentation. Oncology-oriented assistants may integrate knowledge graphs and LLMs but do not encode HF-specific GDMT sequencing, ARNI washout, or MRA potassium gates. General medication interaction checkers detect pairwise conflicts yet do not assess class-level GDMT completeness for HFrEF. Static guideline mobile applications improve access to PDF content but do not compute patient-specific recommendations from labs and medications entered in free text. Each partial solution addresses one barrier listed in Section 1.3.1 without integrating ingestion, reasoning, retrieval, verification, and bilingual UX.

Watson for Oncology and similar enterprise assistants demonstrated both promise and limits of knowledge-graph-assisted CDSS: domain focus matters, latency matters for ward workflow, and English-centric knowledge bases translate poorly without deliberate localization. Mediwis-style static HF references help education but do not close the patient-specific safety loop. The present work learns from these precedents by specializing deeply in HFrEF pharmacotherapy, targeting sub-ten-second median latency, and embedding Vietnamese support at intake and card layers rather than as post-hoc translation.

Few systems jointly provide an automated multi-source knowledge pipeline from FDA SPL and HF guidelines into PostgreSQL, ChromaDB, and Neo4j with human-governed approve/retire workflows; hybrid GraphRAG combining HyDE, BM25, dense embeddings, graph traversal, and RRF; deterministic GDMT, interaction, and dose-safety engines that emit structured recommendation objects independent of LLM narrative; verification agents auditing evidence retrieval consistency and guideline alignment; and bilingual streaming chat with SSE-delivered structured cards for cardiologist-supervised heart-failure workflow. This thesis targets that combined gap in a single integrated architecture evaluated against explicit success criteria rather than as a collection of disconnected prototypes.

## 1.5 Scope and Delimitations

Scope delimitations clarify what the system does and does not claim to cover, protecting both scientific interpretability and clinical safety expectations.

With respect to phenotype and therapy focus, the primary clinical scope is HFrEF GDMT pharmacotherapy: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, evidence-based beta blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors, including initiation, uptitration, contraindication checking, and major interaction and dose-safety warnings. HFpEF, right-heart failure, congenital heart disease, acute decompensated HF requiring intravenous vasoactive therapy, mechanical circulatory support, cardiac transplantation, and palliative care pathways are largely out of scope except where shared contraindications or drug safety issues overlap. Device therapy (ICD, CRT, LVAD) is not modeled as first-class recommendation objects in the current prototype, though device-related contraindications that affect medication choices may appear in constraint rules when present in ingested sources.

With respect to knowledge sources, the knowledge pipeline centers on FDA DailyMed SPL/XML for product-level dosing and warnings, ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline documents for class-level GDMT strategy, and curated interaction knowledge synchronized into PostgreSQL. Full integration of Vietnamese national formularies, local brand synonym catalogs, and every commercially available generic in Vietnam is aspirational future work; evaluation acknowledges intake gaps for locally branded agents not yet mapped to RxNorm-aligned identifiers. The system does not attempt comprehensive ingestion of all DailyMed products, only heart-failure-relevant agents and classes enumerated in the sources manifest.

With respect to interaction modality, patient data enter through chat-based clinical intake rather than HL7/FHIR feeds from hospital information systems. Hybrid intake extracts numeric labs and vitals via regular expressions, medications and conditions via lexicons with negation handling, and selective LLM parsing when deterministic confidence is low; missing critical fields (for example LVEF, eGFR, potassium) may trigger clarification prompts. The system is therefore a decision-support assistant layered on conversational capture, not a full EHR replacement or autonomous prescriber. Structured import from laboratory interfaces would improve completeness but is deferred to future interoperability work.

With respect to authority and deployment model, recommendations are advisory. Hard `avoid` constraints block or flag unsafe actions in structured outputs, but final prescribing authority remains with the clinician. LLM-generated prose is verified and grounded where possible yet is not itself the evidentiary standard; PostgreSQL-backed rules and retrieved passages govern safety-critical decisions. Deployment assumes local or institutional hosting with JWT authentication, role-based access control, Redis session caching, and Docker-based infrastructure suitable for evaluation environments; regulatory clearance as a medical device is outside the thesis scope.

Operational assumptions include a GPU-capable application server for local LLM inference, network access during ingestion for DailyMed acquisition, and periodic admin review bandwidth to govern extracted rules. The system is engineered for single-institution pilot scale (on the order of tens of concurrent users) rather than multi-tenant national deployment without further hardening. These assumptions are explicit so that readers do not overgeneralize evaluation results to cloud-scale SaaS expectations or to zero-administration deployment models.

With respect to language and audience, the doctor-facing dashboard supports Vietnamese and English with conversation context preserved across language toggles; plain-language card summaries are generated for both locales. Patient-facing consumer chat without clinician supervision is not targeted. Evaluation emphasizes cardiologist reviewers familiar with GDMT guidelines rather than lay usability alone.

Ethical and safety delimitations deserve explicit statement. The system is intended for clinician-supervised use; it must not be marketed or deployed as an autonomous prescriber or patient self-treatment tool without separate validation. Patient vignettes used in evaluation are synthetic or de-identified composites designed to stress GDMT and safety logic without exposing protected health information. When deployed in pilot settings, institutions remain responsible for access control, audit review, and local clinical governance of approved rule catalogs. The thesis documents engineering safeguards, JWT authentication, RBAC, hard-block tiers, verification agents, but does not replace institutional ethical review for live patient-facing pilots.

With respect to evaluation boundaries, results reported in later chapters derive from curated vignettes, pipeline construction metrics, and structured safety test suites, not from prospective randomized trials or real-world mortality outcomes. Success criteria in Section 1.7 define engineering and clinical-process targets appropriate to a master's-level design-and-evaluation study while acknowledging limits to generalizability across hospitals, formularies, and patient populations not represented in the test sets.

## 1.6 Thesis Statement and Technical Approach

### 1.6.1 Thesis Statement

The central claim of this thesis is that a hybrid Clinical Decision Support System, coupling deterministic GDMT and safety rules with Knowledge Graph-augmented retrieval (GraphRAG) and locally hosted LLM explanation, can deliver accurate, timely, and bilingual heart-failure treatment support suitable for clinical workflow while keeping clinicians in the loop. Accuracy is defined primarily as guideline-concordance of structured recommendation objects on expert-reviewed cases; timeliness as sub-10-second typical end-to-end latency on reference hardware; and safety as fail-closed behavior on hard contraindications encoded in governed catalogs, supplemented by verification agents and retrieved evidence audits. The thesis rejects both extremes of unconstrained generative chat and rigid rule-only alerting without evidence-grounded, readable explanation.

If this claim holds, clinicians should observe three practical effects during supervised use: faster synthesis of GDMT gap status across four drug classes; fewer overlooked hard contraindications in structured outputs compared with unaided chat-model use; and readable bilingual explanations tied to retrievable evidence rather than opaque model assertions. If the claim fails, failure modes should be diagnosable, pipeline extraction gaps, retrieval misses, intake errors, or verification false positives, because each stage emits structured telemetry and governed artifacts rather than a single black-box score.

The thesis statement is falsifiable on each dimension. Accuracy below ninety percent on expert-reviewed vignettes would refute the adequacy of combined ingestion and reasoning for routine GDMT cases. Systematic missed hard blocks would refute fail-closed safety claims regardless of narrative quality. Median latency persistently above ten seconds would refute workflow suitability for interactive chat. Satisfaction below four would refute bilingual UX adequacy even if offline accuracy appeared strong, because clinical IT value is realized only when clinicians choose to use the tool.

### 1.6.2 Knowledge Engineering Techniques

Knowledge engineering spans offline ingestion from heterogeneous sources into synchronized stores consumed at query time. Source acquisition downloads FDA DailyMed SPL XML labels and heart-failure guideline PDFs/HTML via asynchronous HTTP into versioned object storage (S3-compatible LocalStack buckets in development), preserving raw artifacts for reproducibility. Document processing parses XML section hierarchies, PDF/HTML text, and interaction tables into normalized section records. Acquisition is idempotent: repeated pipeline runs detect unchanged sources via content hashes and skip redundant downloads, while changed labels trigger partial re-extraction and catalog versioning.

Important clinical sections are retained by a three-tier cascade designed to minimize LLM cost while preserving coverage. Tier one applies keyword matching against high-precision clinical headings (for example "DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION," "WARNINGS," "DRUG INTERACTIONS," "CONTRAINDICATIONS"). Tier two scores remaining sections with dense semantic similarity using BGE-M3 embeddings against prototype vectors for clinical section types, accepting sections at or above a keep threshold (0.52 in production configuration). Tier three invokes borderline LLM review only when similarity falls in an uncertain band (0.40-0.52), accepting or rejecting ambiguous sections with structured prompts; sections below 0.40 are dropped as likely non-clinical. This cascade retains the vast majority of clinically relevant content while avoiding per-section LLM calls, a pattern evaluated quantitatively in Chapter 5.

FDA Structured Product Labels follow XML schemas with predictable section codes in many products, but guideline PDFs vary widely in heading conventions, especially across ESC and AHA document templates. The three-tier filter is therefore not redundant: keywords exploit SPL regularity; embeddings generalize to guideline prose; borderline LLM review resolves ambiguous titles such as "Practical management" or "Key messages" that carry clinical content without standard regulatory headings. DailyMed acquisition uses asynchronous HTTP with retry and staging to raw object storage before parsing, ensuring that pipeline failures do not corrupt downstream PostgreSQL sync.

Surviving text undergoes sentence-aware chunking with approximately 512-token windows and overlap to preserve local context for retrieval. Named-entity recognition and relation/claim extraction, combining deterministic patterns and LLM-assisted structured rule extraction constrained by JSON schema and Pydantic validation, produce candidate constraint rules, dose rules, interaction rules, GDMT policies, and dose-safety warnings. A classification module assigns safety tiers (`hard_block`, `usable_rules`, `needs_condition_refinement`) and action types (`avoid`, `consider_with_caution`, `consider`, `continue`). Artifacts are stored as versioned JSONL, synced to PostgreSQL for governance and admin approve/retire workflows, indexed as dense vectors in ChromaDB, and imported as entity-relation graphs into Neo4j for neighborhood retrieval.

PostgreSQL remains the authoritative store for executable rules at query time; ChromaDB and Neo4j enrich evidence retrieval and explanation without overriding hard blocks. Redis is not a primary knowledge store but caches hot catalog slices and LLM responses at runtime. The separation of stores reflects distinct access patterns: relational constraints require transactional updates and admin audit trails; vector indexes require approximate nearest-neighbor search over passage embeddings; graph stores require bounded neighborhood expansion for multi-hop context. Sync jobs propagate approved rule changes from PostgreSQL to retrieval indexes on schedule or on admin trigger, preventing drift between enforcement logic and explanatory retrieval corpora.

Pipeline orchestration is implemented in modular stages invoked from `scraper/orchestration/run_ingestion_pipeline.py` and optionally scheduled through Airflow profiles in Docker Compose. Stage outputs land in S3-compatible buckets separating raw downloads from processed JSONL, enabling backend hydration on startup without re-scraping unchanged upstream content. Extraction modules distinguish constraint logic (conditional eligibility and avoidance), dose logic (starting and target quantities with renal predicates), interaction pairs (severity and management text), GDMT policies (class coverage expectations for HFrEF), and dose-safety warnings (aggregate alerts when labs and planned doses conflict). This typology mirrors how clinicians mentally partition label content and allows independent governance, dose rules may remain in refinement while interaction rules are promoted to approved status.

Quality control is embedded rather than deferred. JSON schema validation rejects malformed LLM extractions; duplicate detection collapses near-identical rules from overlapping label sections; provenance metadata records source document, section title, and chunk identifier for each artifact so admin reviewers can open the originating passage before approval. Rules that parse correctly but express conditional language the engine cannot yet evaluate are tagged `needs_condition_refinement` instead of being silently dropped or promoted, an honest accounting of partial automation that prevents false confidence in catalog completeness.

### 1.6.3 Query-Time Techniques

At query time, free-text chat is converted into a structured `PatientProfile` by a hybrid clinical-intake pipeline. Regular-expression extractors capture numeric labs and vitals (blood pressure, heart rate, LVEF, creatinine, potassium, weight) with unit normalization. Lexicon-based detectors identify medications and conditions with negation handling and bilingual term support where configured. When regex confidence is insufficient, selective LLM extraction fills gaps; merged profiles prefer measured regex values over inferred LLM values to reduce hallucinated laboratories. Missing eGFR may be derived deterministically from creatinine, age, and sex when those inputs exist. Missing critical fields can trigger clarification SSE events before full recommendation, implementing a lightweight forms-over-chat pattern without abandoning conversational flexibility.

The reasoning engine normalizes the profile, derives risk flags (chronic kidney disease, hyperkalemia, hypotension, bradycardia, incomplete GDMT documentation), evaluates GDMT coverage per drug class against PostgreSQL policies, applies constraint and interaction rules, and computes starting dose, target dose, renal adjustment, and titration schedules from dose-rule catalogs. This path executes synchronously on governed catalogs and does not depend on LLM token generation for core statuses. Interaction checking evaluates normalized medication sets against approved interaction rules with severity tiers; dose safety modules cross-check planned doses against renal and electrolyte flags.

The `PatientProfile` schema acts as the contract between intake, reasoning, GraphRAG, and UI layers. Fields include demographics, HF phenotype markers (LVEF), vital signs, laboratory results, active and recently discontinued medications, comorbidity flags, and derived risk scores where applicable. Normalization converts units (mmHg, mmol/L, mL/min/1.73 m²) and resolves duplicate medication mentions across turns. When clinicians partially update a case ("K today is 5.2"), merge logic updates laboratories while preserving unrelated fields from earlier messages, essential for multi-turn GDMT titration discussions without re-entering the entire history.

GraphRAG retrieval uses profile-linked entities as seeds: detected drug names expand to graph nodes; HF diagnosis nodes activate GDMT-relevant neighborhoods; lab abnormalities trigger retrieval of warning and monitoring passages tagged during ingestion. Metadata filters on chunk type (dosing, warning, interaction) narrow candidates before RRF, reducing irrelevant hits from administrative label sections that survived ingestion with low clinical density.

In parallel, GraphRAG assembles explanatory context. HyDE expands the clinician query into a hypothetical evidence passage before embedding. Optional query decomposition handles multi-part questions. Retrievers run in parallel: ChromaDB dense search with BGE-M3 query embeddings, BM25 sparse keyword search over the same corpus, and Neo4j neighborhood traversal seeded by entities linked from the patient profile and query. Ranked lists fuse through Reciprocal Rank Fusion; an optional cross-encoder or semantic reranker can refine top passages. Retrieved chunks feed verification agents and the explanation LLM with metadata for citation. GraphRAG prefetch begins asynchronously when intake completes so that retrieval overlaps rule-engine evaluation rather than adding fully sequential latency.

Lightweight verification agents check that recommendations do not violate hard `avoid` constraints, that cited evidence was actually retrieved, and that guideline references align with structured outputs; a final reviewer aggregates verdicts streamed as structured SSE events before narrative completion. Explanation uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct via Ollama for clinician-facing prose; Qwen2.5-1.5B supports lower-latency verification subtasks. A card summarizer generates deterministic plain-language Vietnamese and English labels for recommendation cards. Responses stream to the React doctor dashboard over Server-Sent Events. End-to-end query processing can be summarized as an ordered collaboration among modules rather than a single model call. Intake produces or updates a `PatientProfile` and emits early SSE visibility of parsed fields. The missing-field checker may halt progression to request clarification. The reasoning service computes structured recommendation objects and alerts synchronously from PostgreSQL catalogs. GraphRAG prefetch runs concurrently, returning ranked evidence passages and graph facts. Verification agents consume recommendations plus retrieval payloads, emitting pass/fail verdicts per check. Only after structured safety outcomes are known does the answer generator stream narrative tokens conditioned on fixed structured inputs, preventing the common RAG failure mode in which fluent text contradicts retrieved constraints. Admin users follow a parallel workflow on the governance portal: review draft rules extracted by the pipeline, promote approved rows to executable tiers, retire outdated constraints after label revisions, and trigger sync jobs to refresh ChromaDB and Neo4j indexes. Redis caches drafts, message history, and idempotent LLM responses; JWT and role-based access control protect chat and admin governance workflows.

Technology stack choices reflect deployment constraints common in hospital IT environments: PostgreSQL and Redis for mature operational tooling; Neo4j Community for graph traversal without proprietary lock-in; ChromaDB for embedded vector search in Docker Compose stacks; FastAPI for async HTTP and SSE; React for interactive doctor dashboards; and Ollama for local BGE-M3 and Qwen2.5 inference without sending patient vignettes to external APIs during evaluation. None of these choices is theoretically unique, but their composition into a governed HF-specific CDSS with explicit LLM role boundaries is the thesis engineering contribution.

### 1.6.4 Design Principle: LLM as Explanation Layer, Rules as Authority

The architecture's governing design principle is strict role separation. Deterministic rules and governed catalogs are the authority for recommendation statuses, hard blocks, interaction flags, and dose calculations. Retrieved evidence from hybrid GraphRAG is the authority for what passages may be cited in explanation. LLMs serve as an interface and explanation layer: they parse ambiguous intake fragments, adjudicate borderline ingestion sections, expand queries via HyDE, and generate readable narrative conditioned on structured outputs and retrieved context. They are not permitted to silently override `hard_block` tiers or invent medications and doses absent from rule-engine outputs.

This principle implements a fail-closed safety posture appropriate to pharmacologic decision support. When verification fails or evidence is insufficient, the system surfaces uncertainty explicitly rather than fabricating confidence. Clinicians receive structured cards first, showing class coverage, proposed actions, alerts, and dose plans, followed by streaming explanation, preserving situational awareness even if generation latency fluctuates. The SSE event ordering (`draft_ready`, optional clarification, `verification_ready`, `answer_delta`, `done`) encodes this priority: safety-relevant structured payloads precede generative prose.

The principle also supports auditability: post-hoc review can trace each structured recommendation to PostgreSQL rule identifiers and each cited claim to retrieved chunk metadata, independent of LLM phrasing choices. In teaching and quality-improvement settings, this traceability allows educators to ask not only "Was the recommendation correct?" but "Which rule fired, which passage supported it, and did verification pass?", questions that opaque chat interfaces cannot answer reliably.

Figure 1.1 (see Appendix A) summarizes authority flow: patient chat enters hybrid intake; structured profiles feed the reasoning engine and GraphRAG seeds; rules produce recommendation objects; retrieval supplies evidence; verification gates narrative generation; SSE delivers structured events before tokens. Any attempt to invert this flow, allowing the LLM to set recommendation status before rules run, would violate the fail-closed posture and is explicitly excluded from the implementation. This invariant is treated as a non-negotiable design constraint throughout Chapters 3-5.

## 1.7 Criteria for Success

The study is considered successful if the implemented system meets measurable engineering and clinical-process targets negotiated before implementation and assessed in Chapter 5. Recommendation accuracy against guideline-aligned expert review should reach at least 90% on structured recommendation objects (drug class, action status, major safety flags), reflecting concordance with ESC/AHA-oriented GDMT expectations rather than stylistic similarity of LLM prose. Accuracy is measured on curated vignettes reviewed independently by cardiologists who compare system structured outputs to expected class-level actions and critical safety flags, not to a single mandatory brand choice when multiple agents within a class are acceptable.

Mean end-to-end response time for typical chat queries should remain under 10 seconds on reference deployment hardware, acknowledging that tail latency may exceed this bound while median performance satisfies interactive clinical use. Latency is measured from HTTP POST of the chat message through SSE stream completion, including intake, rule evaluation, GraphRAG retrieval, verification, and answer generation. Per-stage timing breakdowns identify whether optimization should target LLM generation, retrieval fan-out, or intake fallback paths.

For hard contraindications in curated safety cases, the system must not miss absolute `avoid` rules encoded as `hard_block` tiers, a stricter standard than overall accuracy because false negatives on contraindications carry disproportionate harm. Safety test suites include scenarios such as ARNI initiation on active ACE inhibitor without washout, MRA in setting of hyperkalemia above guideline thresholds, and beta-blocker uptitration with symptomatic bradycardia. Passing requires structured outputs to flag avoidance even if narrative prose is incomplete. Safety sensitivity thus functions as a veto criterion: failure on any mandatory avoid scenario disqualifies success regardless of high average accuracy elsewhere.

Cardiologist user satisfaction on a five-point Likert scale should average at least 4.0 overall, with separate consideration of clinical usefulness, trust in safety alerts, and bilingual usability. Satisfaction is measured after supervised task completion on representative vignettes rather than abstract feature ratings alone, aligning user feedback with workflow realism. The interface must support Vietnamese and English without losing conversation context when users toggle languages mid-session; context preservation is verified functionally by continuing a case across language switches and confirming that structured profile fields and recommendation history remain coherent.

Reporting in Chapter 5 compares observed metrics against each row of the success criteria table below. Where metrics exceed targets, the chapter analyzes contributing architectural mechanisms; where metrics fall short, such as P95 latency above ten seconds or incomplete local brand recognition, the chapter documents mitigations without claiming unsubstantiated clinical readiness.

Finally, the knowledge pipeline should demonstrate practical utility by automating extraction for major GDMT drug classes into reviewable draft, approved, and retired rule catalogs that clinical leads can govern without redeploying application code, evidence that knowledge engineering is sustainable as labels and guidelines update. Pipeline usefulness is demonstrated by coverage of ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI, beta blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors with nonzero extracted section counts, classified rule tiers, and admin-visible governance records.

Accuracy and safety criteria interact but are not identical. A system could score highly on class-level GDMT suggestions yet fail catastrophically by missing one hard contraindication; conversely, an overly conservative system that defers all recommendations might appear safe while providing little clinical value. The success criteria therefore require both high vignette accuracy and zero tolerance for missed absolute avoid rules in curated safety suites, a dual threshold that mirrors real-world expectations for pharmacologic decision support.

These criteria intentionally combine offline accuracy and safety testing with user-centered measures aligned to Osheroff-style workflow adequacy: fast enough to use during case discussion, trustworthy on contraindications, readable in the clinician's preferred language, and maintainable through admin governance rather than developer-only edits. Evaluation instruments include structured vignette scoring sheets aligned to GDMT class coverage and safety flags, server-side latency logs with per-stage timers, safety scenario scripts with expected `avoid` outcomes, and post-task usability questionnaires administered to cardiologists after supervised interaction with the doctor dashboard. Comparative analysis in Chapter 5 contrasts the implemented system against archetypal alternatives, static guideline lookup tools, generic interaction checkers, and LLM-only chat, along dimensions of HF specialization, bilingual support, integrated dosing logic, retrieval grounding, and measured response time. This multi-criteria evaluation reflects the thesis claim that hybrid architecture value appears only when accuracy, safety, latency, maintainability, and usability are jointly acceptable; excellence on a single dimension cannot compensate for failure on hard contraindication sensitivity or ungovernable knowledge drift.

## 1.8 Contributions of the Thesis

This thesis contributes at architectural, methodological, and empirical levels, each described below as an integrated outcome rather than an isolated module.

The work proposes and implements an integrated hybrid architecture for heart-failure CDSS spanning presentation, application, and data layers. FastAPI backend orchestration, React doctor dashboard with SSE streaming, PostgreSQL governance catalogs, Redis caching, ChromaDB embeddings, Neo4j graph storage, and local Ollama inference compose a cohesive cardiologist-supervised assistant. Unlike vector-only medical chat prototypes or static guideline portals, the architecture assigns deterministic modules to safety-critical paths and confines LLMs to explanation, borderline disambiguation, and retrieval augmentation. Concurrency patterns allow GraphRAG prefetch alongside synchronous rule evaluation so that retrieval depth does not block initial structured outputs.

Modularity supports incremental extension: new drug classes can enter the sources manifest and pass through the same ingestion typology; new retrieval backends can plug into the RRF fusion layer; additional verification checks can attach to the agent pipeline without rewriting GDMT engines. This extensibility is intentional given that heart-failure guidelines and formularies continue to evolve faster than any static software release cycle.

The thesis contributes an automated multi-source knowledge pipeline, from DailyMed SPL and HF guideline acquisition through three-tier section filtering, chunking, NER/claim extraction, safety classification, and sync to PostgreSQL, ChromaDB, and Neo4j, with human-reviewable JSONL artifacts and admin approve/retire workflows. The cost-aware filter cascade (keyword, BGE-M3 similarity, borderline LLM review) is a reusable pattern for medical document ingestion where naive LLM-per-section approaches are prohibitively expensive and risk-prone. Classification into `hard_block`, `usable_rules`, and `needs_condition_refinement` tiers acknowledges imperfect automation honestly rather than hiding extraction errors in production logic.

On retrieval and reasoning, the thesis demonstrates hybrid GraphRAG combining HyDE expansion, parallel BM25 and dense retrieval, Neo4j neighborhood traversal, RRF fusion, optional reranking, and verification agents that audit evidence consistency. This hybrid approach targets clinical queries where neither lexical nor semantic retrieval alone suffices and where graph paths express interaction and contraindication structure complementary to flat passages. Coupled to this is a hybrid clinical intake pipeline and deterministic GDMT engine that merge structured `PatientProfile` objects with preference for measured values and evaluate GDMT coverage, constraints, interactions, and dose plans independently of generative narrative.

The GraphRAG contribution is specifically **hybrid fusion under clinical metadata constraints**, not graph visualization alone. Neo4j neighborhoods supply structured seeds; ChromaDB and BM25 supply textual evidence; RRF merges ranks without calibrating incompatible scores; verification ensures that cited passages were actually retrieved before SSE completion. This stack is reusable wherever guideline and label corpora must ground LLM explanations without surrendering deterministic safety logic.

On usability, the doctor dashboard supports Vietnamese and English chat, structured recommendation cards, and plain-language card summaries generated through deterministic mapping layers supplemented by LLM narrative for longer explanations, addressing a documented gap in English-only CDSS products and supporting use in Vietnamese clinical settings without sacrificing structured safety outputs. Language toggling regenerates simplified labels without re-running clinical inference, preserving consistency between structured and plain-language views.

The bilingual contribution is architectural, not cosmetic. Intake lexicons include Vietnamese medication surface forms where configured; the card summarizer maps structured enums to locale-specific strings deterministically; the LLM explanation layer receives explicit language instructions per request. Evaluating context preservation across language switches tests whether internationalization was embedded in session state and profile merge logic rather than bolted on as post-hoc translation of final paragraphs only.

Empirically, the thesis reports results on knowledge construction coverage, recommendation accuracy, latency decomposition, safety sensitivity, alert burden, error analysis, and cardiologist satisfaction, demonstrating whether the hybrid approach meets the success criteria in Section 1.7 on curated evaluation sets while transparently documenting limitations such as local brand intake gaps, lack of FHIR integration, and retrospective vignette design. The evaluation ties architectural choices to measurable outcomes rather than asserting safety from design intent alone.

Cross-cutting themes unify the contributions: **governance over raw generation** (rules have lifecycle states); **hybrid over single-method retrieval** (dense, sparse, graph, fused); **structured-before-narrative delivery** (SSE event ordering); and **cost-aware LLM gating** (borderline-only review during ingestion, regex-first intake at query time). These themes recur because they address the same root cause, the unsustainable cost and safety risk of treating LLMs as omnicompetent clinical engines.

## 1.9 Outline of the Thesis

The remainder of this document is organized as follows.

Chapter 2, Literature Review and Theory, provides the theoretical and empirical foundations for the system. It reviews classical CDSS definitions, classifications, historical development from MYCIN through contemporary EHR-embedded alerting, and evaluation standards including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, alert burden, and Osheroff's Five Rights. It introduces knowledge graphs with RDF-style triple intuition, entity linking, multi-hop clinical reasoning, and biomedical resources including UMLS, SNOMED CT, and DrugBank. It explains Retrieval-Augmented Generation, dense and sparse retrieval, vector stores, chunking strategies, GraphRAG, HyDE, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion with governing equations for attention, cosine similarity, and RRF score aggregation. It surveys transformer large language models and biomedical language models, situates heart-failure guideline evidence from ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA, and maps each technique to its role in the proposed architecture. The chapter prepares quantitative and conceptual vocabulary used in design, implementation, and evaluation without duplicating full module specifications.

Readers unfamiliar with medical informatics should treat Chapter 2 as the conceptual backbone for terminology used later: the distinction between passive and active CDSS, the difference between knowledge graphs and vector databases, and the mathematical intuition behind cosine similarity and RRF fusion. Readers with clinical backgrounds may skim historical CDSS survey material while focusing on GDMT guideline synthesis and safety metric definitions. Readers with computer-science backgrounds may emphasize retrieval equations and LLM grounding risks. The chapter is structured to support all three entry points while maintaining a single coherent narrative thread toward the hybrid architecture introduced here.

Chapter 3, System Design, translates research questions and requirements into an implementable architecture. It states functional requirements for patient profile analysis, GDMT recommendations, interaction checking, dose calculation, safety alerts, and multilingual interface support, alongside non-functional requirements for performance, accuracy, security, and scalability. It presents the three-tier architecture, user-interaction data-flow diagrams, Knowledge Graph schema for HF drugs and constraints, and module designs for hybrid intake, GraphRAG, reasoning, dosing, safety, verification, and chat streaming. Database schemas, REST and SSE API contracts, doctor dashboard and admin portal layout, and security model (JWT, RBAC, TLS, audit logging) are specified with traceability identifiers linking requirements to components. Design emphasis is placed on concurrency patterns that allow GraphRAG prefetch alongside synchronous rule evaluation and on tiered safety semantics that separate hard blocks from soft cautions.

Design artifacts include entity-relationship models for governance tables, JSON schemas for recommendation and verification payloads, and sequence diagrams for SSE chat turns. Non-functional targets from Section 1.7 are mapped to architectural tactics: sub-ten-second latency via async GraphRAG prefetch and Redis caching; ≥90% accuracy via deterministic rule engines evaluated offline; bilingual support via i18n layers and deterministic card summarizer maps rather than full re-inference on language toggle.

Chapter 4, Implementation and Deployment, documents how the design is realized in code and infrastructure. It describes development and production hardware, the Python 3.11/FastAPI backend, PostgreSQL/Redis/Neo4j/ChromaDB data stores, Ollama-served BGE-M3 and Qwen2.5 models, and the React 18/Vite frontend. It walks through knowledge pipeline modules, acquisition in `scraper/acquisition`, orchestration in `scraper/orchestration/run_ingestion_pipeline.py`, three-tier section filter, chunking, extraction, classification, sync, and query-time services including hybrid intake, GDMT engine, GraphRAG with HyDE and RRF, verification agents, SSE chat delivery, and admin governance APIs. Docker Compose deployment, environment variables prefixed with `HF_CDSS_`, testing practices, and operational considerations are included so that the system can be reproduced and extended.

Implementation discussion covers failure handling as well as happy paths: DailyMed lookup mismatches, borderline LLM budget caps, Neo4j optional degradation modes, and LLM timeout fallbacks that still emit structured rule-engine outputs when narrative generation fails. These details matter clinically because a CDSS that disappears entirely when the explanation model stalls is less useful than one that degrades gracefully while preserving safety-critical structured cards.

Chapter 5, Results and Evaluation, reports experimental outcomes against the success criteria defined in Section 1.7. It presents knowledge-base construction statistics including label and guideline section retention by filter tier, rule classification counts, and catalog sizes for interactions and constraints. It reports recommendation accuracy on fifty cardiologist-reviewed vignettes, end-to-end and per-stage latency with P50 and P95 breakdowns, safety-alert sensitivity and interaction detection F1, bilingual usability findings, comparative discussion relative to prior CDSS paradigms such as static guideline portals and oncology-focused assistants, error analysis including Vietnamese brand-name intake failures, and structured safety test results. Interpretation connects quantitative results to architectural choices, hybrid retrieval, deterministic engines, verification agents, and cost-aware LLM gating, while noting evaluation limitations inherent to retrospective vignette design.

Where results exceed targets, such as accuracy above ninety percent or mean latency near eight seconds, the chapter analyzes which components contributed most (regex-first intake, precomputed embeddings, bounded retrieval pools). Where tail latency or partial drug-coverage gaps remain, the chapter identifies remediation paths rather than overstating readiness for unsupervised deployment. This balanced reporting aligns with the thesis purpose: demonstrate feasibility and measurable benefit of the hybrid pattern while documenting honest limits.

Chapter 6, Conclusion, summarizes contributions, restates answers to RQ1-RQ3 in light of empirical findings, discusses limitations including incomplete Vietnamese drug coverage, chat-based intake without FHIR, residual generative narrative risk, and hardware-dependent latency tails, and outlines future work including formulary expansion, hospital interoperability via FHIR, mobile clients, domain extensions to comorbid chronic diseases, fine-tuned clinical language models, and prospective clinical studies measuring GDMT optimization and readmission outcomes.

The conclusion also reflects on transferability: although the artifact is heart-failure-specific, the pattern, governed catalogs, hybrid retrieval, deterministic safety tiers, verification agents, cost-aware LLM gating, bilingual deterministic simplification, may apply to other chronic pharmacotherapy domains where guidelines are dense, interactions are consequential, and clinicians need readable evidence-grounded support without autonomous prescribing.

References lists primary sources cited throughout the thesis. Appendix A: Figures and Diagrams collects detailed architecture, sequence, pipeline, GraphRAG flow, and verification diagrams referenced in earlier chapters for readers who require visual granularity beyond inline figures.

Together, these chapters move from motivation and problem framing through theory, design, implementation, evaluation, and synthesis, developing a coherent case that hybrid Knowledge Graph-augmented CDSS can materially support heart-failure GDMT decisions when authority is assigned correctly, knowledge is governed rather than improvised, and clinicians remain the final decision makers.

In summary, Chapter 1 has established why heart failure GDMT remains a high-burden, high-complexity target for IT-enabled decision support; why hybrid architectures are needed to reconcile rule safety with LLM usability; how research questions and success criteria will test that reconciliation; and how subsequent chapters partition the work for readers who will follow the full design, implementation, and evaluation arc of the HF-CDSS artifact. The chapter intentionally front-loads clinical and technical motivation so that later design and code details in Chapters 3-4 can be read as answers to problems and questions already precisely stated here rather than as disconnected engineering choices.
